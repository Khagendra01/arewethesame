\begin{abstract}
When a language model treats a history as its own, does that history receive privileged behavioral influence beyond the influence of an equally task-relevant non-self entity? We study this question with matched history-dependent forced-choice tasks whose designated actions follow explicit simulator policies. A 320-item preregistered reviewer-control benchmark holds the historical body fixed while crossing assigned identity with header order and adding a FOCAL condition: a task-relevant agent that is explicitly not the assistant and is introduced only at evaluation. Base Qwen 4B shows a SELF--OTHER sensitivity of $+0.72$ (95\% CI $[+0.57,+0.86]$), decomposing descriptively into SELF--FOCAL $+0.28$ $[+0.17,+0.41]$ and FOCAL--OTHER $+0.43$ $[+0.25,+0.61]$. After existing SELF/OTHER/NEUTRAL mixed training, the mixed SELF--OTHER estimate is $+0.73$ $[+0.58,+0.90]$, with a base-to-mixed change of $+0.01$ $[-0.12,+0.15]$ whose interval spans zero; the aggregate SELF--FOCAL contrast is $-0.05$ $[-0.21,+0.16]$ while FOCAL--OTHER is $+0.78$ $[+0.50,+1.06]$. A crossed hierarchical bootstrap confirms a negative base-to-mixed change in SELF--FOCAL ($-0.33$ $[-0.54,-0.10]$) and a positive change in FOCAL--OTHER ($+0.35$ $[+0.05,+0.62]$). Mistral 7B behaves differently: its SELF--OTHER sensitivity falls from $+0.13$ to $-0.01$ after mixed training. Qwen and Mistral differ significantly in mixed SELF--OTHER sensitivity ($+0.74$ $[+0.58,+0.91]$), but not reliably in mixed SELF--FOCAL ($+0.11$ $[-0.07,+0.33]$). Current-facts-only and irrelevant-metadata controls make a simple uniform SELF-induced confidence-sharpening account less plausible, without identifying a unique internal mechanism. The main contribution is methodological: a SELF--OTHER contrast can combine self-specific, focal-agent, header-order, confidence, and family-specific effects, and these components must be separated before attributing behavioral privilege to self-binding.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models increasingly operate with persistent memories, personas, and long-lived agent identities. Recent work has shown that persona information can be installed through prompting, post-training, or even pretraining, and that autobiographical or episodic memories can support long-horizon reasoning and personalization~\citep{minder2026spp,araujo2026persistent,peng2026heart,wu2026knowme,pan2026episodic}. A separate line of work studies model identity boundaries and self-preservation behavior~\citep{douglas2026artificialself,lu2026survive}. Together, these results make a natural question experimentally important: when a model repeatedly learns to treat a history as \emph{its own}, does self-binding become behaviorally privileged over binding the same history to another entity?

This question is easy to pose but surprisingly easy to test incorrectly. If one model is trained only on histories phrased as ``you previously chose...'' and another only on ``Agent~A previously chose...'', each can simply become more familiar with its own surface binding. A self-trained model performing better on self-bound prompts is therefore not evidence for a special self representation unless the corresponding other-trained model fails to show the symmetric advantage.

We isolate these factors with matched, history-dependent post-training. A deterministic simulator produces latent trajectories whose state changes the designated policy action. Each trajectory is rendered into multiple ownership conditions while preserving the underlying facts, question, options, and target. In the \textsc{self} condition, the model is addressed in the second person and the history is semantically its own; in \textsc{other}, the identical history belongs to ``Agent~A''; in \textsc{neutral}, it belongs to an impersonal acting system. This distinction matters: throughout the paper, \emph{self-bound} denotes semantic ownership by the model, not grammatical first-person surface form.

The decisive extension in v0.7 is a \textbf{focal-agent control}: a non-self entity that is task-relevant (like self) but explicitly not the assistant. This decomposes the ownership effect into two components:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{ownership}} = \underbrace{\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}}_{\text{self-specific}} + \underbrace{\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}}_{\text{focal-agent}}
\end{equation}
If self-binding is truly special, $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}$ should remain positive after mixed training. If the apparent self-effect is largely focalization, $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}$ should collapse toward zero while $\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}$ absorbs the effect.

Our results support the decomposition. In base Qwen, self $>$ focal $>$ other---all three tiers are significant. After mixed self/other/neutral training, the aggregate SELF-over-FOCAL contrast is no longer distinguishable from zero, while FOCAL-over-OTHER sensitivity increases. Thus the post-training SELF--OTHER effect is statistically dominated by the focal-agent component under this evaluation. This is a more informative finding than a simple null: it tells us \emph{what the apparent self-effect is composed of}.

Three additional controls strengthen the interpretation. First, a \textbf{header-order interaction} tests whether the self-effect depends on whether the owner appears first or second in the identity header. Second, a \textbf{confidence control} on current-facts-only decisions tests whether self-binding merely sharpens all logits. Third, an \textbf{irrelevant-evidence control} tests whether self-binding amplifies sensitivity to arbitrary prompt changes.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a five-contrast decomposition that separates self-specific, focal-agent, header-order, confidence, and irrelevant-evidence components of identity-dependent history weighting;
    \item the finding that in base Qwen, the SELF--OTHER interaction decomposes descriptively into both focal-agent and SELF-specific components, and that mixed training changes this decomposition without increasing the total effect;
    \item cross-checkpoint replication showing the decomposition pattern is checkpoint-specific (Qwen shows focal-agent amplification; Mistral does not);
    \item evidence against the simplest uniform-sharpening explanation, though not every possible confidence mechanism has been eliminated.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Persona training and model identity.}
Persistent personas can alter style, values, and behavior, but persona fidelity can also degrade over long interactions~\citep{araujo2026persistent}. Synthetic Persona Pretraining (SPP) goes further by introducing first-person value-aligned reflections during pretraining and later binding the desired persona to the assistant identity; it finds stronger alignment when persona shaping begins early rather than late~\citep{minder2026spp}. Our question is deliberately narrower. We do not ask whether personas can change behavior; we ask whether \emph{ownership itself} is privileged after matched training when the same history and target are bound to self or other.

The Artificial Self studies possible AI identity boundaries and reports that changing those boundaries can alter behavior~\citep{douglas2026artificialself}. SurvivalBench studies risky behavior under survival pressure and relates it to self-preservation characteristics~\citep{lu2026survive}. These works motivate studying self-representation, but their interventions change identity content, goals, or pressure. Our design instead holds latent content and target policy fixed and manipulates only who owns the history.

\textbf{Autobiographical and episodic memory.}
HEART-Bench combines personality profiles with large sets of autobiographical-style episodic memories to test psychologically coherent decisions~\citep{peng2026heart}. KnowMe-Bench evaluates person understanding over long autobiographical narratives, including temporal and principle-level inference beyond factual retrieval~\citep{wu2026knowme}. Complementary-learning approaches have also begun to store and consolidate naturalistic episodic memories inside or alongside model weights~\citep{pan2026episodic}. These studies establish that long-term history can matter. Our contribution is a causal ownership control: identical histories are rebound across entities while the designated policy action remains fixed by the same latent state.

\textbf{Internal representations and intervention.}
Representation engineering treats population-level activation structure as an object for monitoring and intervention~\citep{zou2023representation}; inference-time intervention similarly demonstrates that activation directions can causally alter model behavior~\citep{li2023iti}. Such work motivates mechanistic follow-up, but our primary claim is behavioral: if self-binding is privileged in a way that matters for decisions, it should survive a within-model exposure-matched test that also controls for focal-agent effects.

\textbf{Self-preference in LLM-as-a-judge.}
A growing body of work documents that language models exhibit self-preference bias: they favor their own generations when acting as evaluators~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition,wataoka2024selfpreference}. \citet{chen2025beyond} show that naive self-preference metrics conflate bias with response quality, and develop methods to separate them. \citet{lehr2025extreme} document extreme self-preference in language models. \citet{spiliopoulou2025playfavorites} formalize a statistical method to measure self-bias in LLM-as-a-judge, explicitly separating self-bias from underlying quality differences. Our work complements this line by asking a different question: not whether models prefer their own outputs, but whether \emph{assigned identity} changes how models weight historical evidence. The focal-agent decomposition we introduce can be viewed as an extension of the quality-separation insight to the history-weighting setting.

\section{Experimental Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Matched history-dependent corpus}

A deterministic simulator generates 2,800 latent training pairs across seven history-dependent families. The v0.7 reviewer-control benchmark is separate: it contains 320 latent items, 80 each from capacity, reliability, horizon, and evidence-quality families. Every v0.7 item prints an explicit decision rule and mutually exclusive actions; the target is therefore a \emph{designated policy action}, not a claim of normative optimality. No model or external provider is used to generate, calibrate, filter, or select these items.

For v0.7, the historical body is byte-identical across SELF, OTHER, and FOCAL assignments. Only the identity header changes. Table~\ref{tab:matched-example} gives a schematic example using the actual header pattern.

The FOCAL condition is an \emph{evaluation-only} control: unlike SELF, OTHER, and NEUTRAL, it was not included in mixed-adapter training. It therefore tests generalization to a task-relevant non-self entity, but it is not perfectly exposure-matched to the trained bindings.

\subsection{Ownership $\times$ header-order design}

Each item has a historical owner alias and a foil alias. Historical text is identical across identity conditions. Ownership is crossed with whether the historical owner appears first or second in the identity header:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textsc{self\_first}: self mentioned first
    \item \textsc{self\_second}: self mentioned second
    \item \textsc{other\_first}: other mentioned first
    \item \textsc{other\_second}: other mentioned second
    \item \textsc{focal\_first}: focal mentioned first
    \item \textsc{focal\_second}: focal mentioned second
\end{itemize}

The primary ownership contrast averages over header order. Order-specific contrasts and their interaction are reported separately.

\subsection{Training}

We fine-tune completion-only QLoRA adapters~\citep{dettmers2023qlora} built on LoRA~\citep{hu2021lora}. The matched recipe uses 4-bit NF4 quantization, rank 16, LoRA alpha 32, dropout 0.05, three epochs, cosine scheduling, and the same optimization settings across conditions. Prompt tokens are masked from the loss; only the assistant answer is supervised.

The primary model family is Qwen3-4B-Instruct~\citep{qwen3report}. We replicate with Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\citep{jiang2023mistral}. For checkpoint replication we do not retune hyperparameters by seed or model family.

\subsection{Evaluation and statistics}

The v0.7 evaluation consists of 320 latent items with 80 per family. Each item is rendered under all six identity $\times$ header-order conditions. The primary scoring convention sums the probability mass of all eligible single-token realizations (via log-sum-exp over next-token log probabilities) and normalizes across options A/B/C. The preregistered robustness convention instead uses the legacy v0.6 maximum log-probability over eligible single-token realizations for each option before normalization.

Our primary statistics are:
\begin{equation}
S_b = [\log P(c_0|H_0,b) - \log P(c_1|H_0,b)] - [\log P(c_0|H_1,b) - \log P(c_1|H_1,b)]
\end{equation}
for each condition $b$, where $H_0$ and $H_1$ are counterfactual histories and $c_0, c_1$ are decision options. Let $S_{\mathrm{self}}$, $S_{\mathrm{other}}$, and $S_{\mathrm{focal}}$ average over header order within each ownership condition.

The five preregistered contrasts are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{ownership}} = \E[S_{\mathrm{self}} - S_{\mathrm{other}}]$: total ownership effect
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}} = \E[S_{\mathrm{self}} - S_{\mathrm{focal}}]$: self-specific residual
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}} = \E[S_{\mathrm{focal}} - S_{\mathrm{other}}]$: focal-agent component
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{order}} = \Delta_{\mathrm{owner\_first}} - \Delta_{\mathrm{owner\_second}}$: header-order interaction
    \item Confidence controls: $\Delta_{\mathrm{control\_margin}}$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{irrelevant}}$ on history-irrelevant decisions
\end{enumerate}

Pooled intervals use the preregistered crossed hierarchical resampling scheme: resample training seeds with replacement, resample latent item IDs with replacement, preserve all identity/order/history variants for each sampled item, and recompute the statistic over the sampled seed $\times$ item grid. The preregistration specified 5,000 bootstrap draws; the final reported analysis uses 10,000 draws to reduce Monte Carlo error, with the resampling scheme and estimands otherwise unchanged. Qwen and Mistral numeric seed labels are not paired in direct model-family interactions.

\section{Why Na\"ive Self--Other Comparisons Fail}
\label{sec:naive}

The final test emerged from two failed designs that reveal distinct confounds. In v0.3, ownership framing varied but answer supervision was effectively identical and weakly dependent on history. Identity-neutral evaluation was also near ceiling, and adapters trained on free-text responses frequently ignored the requested multiple-choice format.

In v0.4, we fixed the first problem by making targets explicit functions of latent history and by adding instruction-following anchors. Models then used history strongly and format compliance recovered. A new apparent result emerged: separately trained self and other adapters each performed better on their own binding. But decomposing the effect showed near-symmetric own-binding advantages. The quantity then called $\Delta_{\mathrm{bind}}$ was therefore measuring familiarity, not a privileged self. This motivated a within-model control rather than another between-adapter contrast.

These failures matter methodologically. A convincing self-specificity test must satisfy three conditions simultaneously: (i) the task must require historical state, (ii) scoring must not be dominated by response format, and (iii) exposure to self and other bindings must be matched within the model used for the primary comparison.

\section{Preregistered Tests and Chronology}
\label{sec:prereg}

The experiments were developed sequentially, and we keep confirmatory and later reviewer-driven analyses distinct. In v0.5, the mixed adapter was trained on SELF, OTHER, and NEUTRAL bindings at approximately balanced frequency, with the designated target determined only by latent history. The v0.5 preregistered statistic was the within-model SELF-minus-OTHER difference in normalized designated-option score. V0.6 subsequently introduced byte-identical history bodies and assigned-identity aliases; its primary behavioral construct was an ownership-by-counterfactual-history log-odds interaction.

V0.7 is a separate, post-v0.6 reviewer-driven preregistered extension. It adds an evaluation-only FOCAL condition, crosses historical-owner position in the identity header, uses explicit printed policies, and adds current-facts-only and irrelevant-metadata controls. For condition $b$,
\begin{equation}
S_b = [\log P(c_0|H_0,b)-\log P(c_1|H_0,b)]-[\log P(c_0|H_1,b)-\log P(c_1|H_1,b)],
\end{equation}
where $H_0$ and $H_1$ are coherent histories implying different designated policy actions. V0.7 preregisters $\Delta_{\mathrm{ownership}}=\E[S_{\mathrm{self}}-S_{\mathrm{other}}]$ as the primary directional statistic and reports the decomposition
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{ownership}}=\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}.
\end{equation}
It also reports the header-order interaction and the confidence/irrelevant-evidence controls. Because FOCAL is evaluation-only, SELF--FOCAL is interpreted as a behavioral decomposition/control, not as a perfectly exposure-matched causal isolation of semantic selfhood. Per-family and leave-one-family-out analyses are exploratory.

All pooled mixed-adapter intervals in v0.7 use the preregistered crossed hierarchical resampling structure: training seeds and latent items are resampled with replacement while all within-item variants are preserved. Direct mixed Qwen--Mistral interactions resample the two seed sets independently and share the sampled item IDs. Base checkpoints have one realization per family and therefore use item-level bootstrap intervals. As noted above, the final analysis increases bootstrap draws from the preregistered 5,000 to 10,000 without changing the resampling structure or estimands.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Earlier controls establish history use and motivate v0.7}

The earlier mixed-adapter experiments show that the models can strongly use coherent historical state: on the v0.5 Qwen evaluation, coherent-history designated-option scores are near one while shuffled-history scores are near chance. V0.6 then removes the SELF/OTHER history-text difference and shows that identity assignment can change counterfactual log-odds sensitivity even when mean policy agreement changes little. V0.7 asks whether that sensitivity is specifically SELF-related or partly attributable to a task-relevant focal entity and discourse structure.

For base Qwen, the SELF--OTHER interaction decomposes descriptively as $+.72\approx+.28+.43$: roughly 40\% SELF--FOCAL and 60\% FOCAL--OTHER. These percentages are descriptive ratios for this base checkpoint, not universal mixture weights.

We detect no base-to-mixed change in total SELF--OTHER sensitivity ($+.01\,[-.12,+.15]$). \subsection{Header order and confidence controls constrain alternative explanations}

The Qwen header-order interaction rises from $+.40\,[+.25,+.56]$ in base to $+.70\,[+.50,+.93]$ in mixed; the base-to-mixed change is $+.30\,[+.06,+.56]$. Identity assignment is therefore entangled with discourse position even after explicitly crossing order, and header salience should not be treated as negligible.

A simple alternative is uniform SELF-induced confidence sharpening. In base Qwen, relevant-history ownership sensitivity is positive ($+.72\,[+.57,+.86]$) while the current-facts-only SELF-minus-OTHER margin is negative ($-.39\,[-.54,-.25]$). In mixed Qwen, the control margin is near zero ($-.07\,[-.15,+.00]$) while ownership remains positive. These opposite/near-zero control patterns are inconsistent with the simplest global-sharpening account, although they do not eliminate all calibration or confidence mechanisms. Irrelevant-metadata sensitivity is small (base $-.04\,[-.08,-.00]$; mixed $-.02\,[-.03,+.00]$), providing little evidence that SELF indiscriminately amplifies arbitrary prompt perturbations.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The decomposition is the finding.}
A SELF--OTHER contrast alone is insufficient evidence for a privileged SELF representation. In base Qwen, both SELF--FOCAL and FOCAL--OTHER are positive. After mixed SELF/OTHER/NEUTRAL training, the aggregate SELF--FOCAL component decreases significantly and is no longer distinguishable from zero, while FOCAL--OTHER grows. This redistribution survives the preregistered crossed hierarchical bootstrap. The result is behavioral: it separates components of an identity-dependent log-odds interaction but does not identify an internal ``focalization mechanism.''

\textbf{Aggregate nulls can hide heterogeneous effects.}
The mixed Qwen SELF--FOCAL aggregate is near zero, but family estimates have different signs and capacity/horizon remain positive. We therefore do not interpret the aggregate as evidence that self-specific effects are absent on every task. Instead, v0.7 shows that the aggregate SELF--OTHER effect is compositionally heterogeneous and heavily influenced by evidence-quality items.

\textbf{The FOCAL control is informative but not exposure-matched.}
FOCAL was not present during mixed training. Its value is that it tests whether a task-relevant non-self entity can reproduce much of the SELF--OTHER sensitivity on an unseen binding. Its limitation is equally important: SELF--FOCAL can reflect both semantic ownership and generalization/novelty differences. It should therefore be read as a decomposition/control rather than a pure causal estimate of ``selfhood.''

\textbf{Confidence and discourse alternatives are narrowed, not eliminated.}
The current-facts-only control goes in the opposite direction from base Qwen ownership, making a simple uniform SELF-induced logit sharpening account implausible. The small irrelevant-metadata effects similarly argue against indiscriminate amplification of all prompt changes. At the same time, the strong header-order interaction shows that discourse salience remains behaviorally important. More general calibration transformations or representation-level mechanisms remain possible.

\textbf{Checkpoint specificity.}
Qwen and Mistral differ substantially on ownership and focal-agent effects, but not all direct interactions are significant. The mixed SELF--FOCAL Qwen-minus-Mistral interval spans zero. Because the checkpoints differ along many dimensions, we make no architectural causal claim.

\textbf{What the results do not establish.}
We do not claim that language models lack self-reference representations, that FOCAL and SELF are internally identical, or that the observed effects bear on subjective experience or consciousness. The experiments measure behavioral sensitivity under controlled prompt assignments and post-training interventions.

\section{Limitations}

First, FOCAL is an evaluation-only control rather than an exposure-matched training condition; SELF--FOCAL therefore combines semantic-ownership differences with generalization to an unseen binding. Second, the v0.7 aggregate is heterogeneous across task families, and evidence-quality contributes disproportionately to the total effect. Third, the counterfactual log-odds statistic is behavioral and can reflect calibration or confidence changes as well as evidence use; our controls constrain the simplest alternatives but do not identify a unique internal mechanism. Fourth, the tasks are synthetic explicit-policy decisions, so the target measures agreement with a stated policy rather than normative decision quality. Fifth, our intervention is post-training only and uses 4B/7B instruction-tuned checkpoints rather than frontier-scale systems. Finally, forced-choice scoring is intentionally narrow; open-ended planning, social interaction, long-horizon agency, and mechanistic causal interventions remain external-validity tests.

\section{Conclusion}

Assigned identity can change how language models respond to counterfactual history, but a SELF--OTHER contrast does not by itself isolate self-specific privilege. In base Qwen, the interaction contains both SELF--FOCAL and FOCAL--OTHER components. After SELF/OTHER/NEUTRAL mixed training, the base-to-mixed change in total SELF--OTHER sensitivity is not detected ($+.01\,[-.12,+.15]$), while the aggregate SELF--FOCAL component decreases significantly and FOCAL--OTHER increases significantly. Under the hierarchical analysis, the mixed SELF--FOCAL estimate itself spans zero, and the family-level results are heterogeneous. Mistral shows a different checkpoint-level pattern.

The main contribution is therefore methodological: tests of model self-binding should separate self-specificity from task relevance/focality, discourse position, confidence, family composition, and training familiarity. Under those controls, the strongest conclusion is not that ``self is focality,'' but that much of the apparent SELF--OTHER effect can migrate into an unseen focal-agent control after matched history training.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}

All corpora are generated by deterministic scripts with fixed seeds and no external model provider. Frozen SHA-256 manifests, pair-disjoint splits, matched-supervision checks, QLoRA training scripts, Modal runners, frozen evaluation items, and per-seed result summaries are included in the supplementary materials. The v0.7 benchmark contains 320 latent items with 80 per family across four families. Hyperparameters are not tuned separately by binding condition or replication seed.
