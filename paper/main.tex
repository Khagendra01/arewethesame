\begin{abstract}
We ask whether autobiographical post-training creates a behavioral privilege for histories bound to a language model's own identity, beyond ordinary familiarity with an entity representation. We construct matched history-dependent decision tasks in which latent events, consequences, questions, and correct targets are fixed while only ownership binding changes: the same history is rendered as belonging to the model (addressed as ``you''), to a third-party agent, or impersonally. Naive comparisons between separately trained adapters produce apparent own-binding advantages, but these are symmetric and therefore confound self-specificity with binding familiarity. To remove that confound, we preregister a mixed-binding adapter trained equally on self, other, and neutral bindings and evaluate the within-model statistic $\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}$, the self-minus-other difference in correct-option probability. History use is strong, yet the preregistered in-distribution effect is essentially zero. Five-seed replications with Qwen 4B and Mistral 7B preserve the null (pooled $+0.0017$ and $-0.0002$, respectively). On 700 out-of-distribution items with new templates and task families, small self-favoring effects appear in isolated seeds but do not pool reliably in either architecture. The experiments separate history use, entity binding, binding familiarity, and self-specificity, showing that the first three can be learned without a robust behavioral privilege for self-bound autobiography under this post-training paradigm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models increasingly operate with persistent memories, personas, and long-lived agent identities. Recent work has shown that persona information can be installed through prompting, post-training, or even pretraining, and that autobiographical or episodic memories can support long-horizon reasoning and personalization~\citep{minder2026spp,araujo2026persistent,peng2026heart,wu2026knowme,pan2026episodic}. A separate line of work studies model identity boundaries and self-preservation behavior~\citep{douglas2026artificialself,lu2026survive}. Together, these results make a natural question experimentally important: when a model repeatedly learns to treat a history as \emph{its own}, does self-binding become behaviorally privileged over binding the same history to another entity?

This question is easy to pose but surprisingly easy to test incorrectly. If one model is trained only on histories phrased as ``you previously chose...'' and another only on ``Agent~A previously chose...'', each can simply become more familiar with its own surface binding. A self-trained model performing better on self-bound prompts is therefore not evidence for a special self representation unless the corresponding other-trained model fails to show the symmetric advantage. Likewise, a model may memorize answer style without using history at all, or a benchmark may saturate before any binding effect can be measured.

We isolate these factors with matched, history-dependent post-training. A deterministic simulator produces latent trajectories whose state changes the correct decision. Each trajectory is rendered into multiple ownership conditions while preserving the underlying facts, question, options, and target. In the \textsc{self} condition, the model is addressed in the second person and the history is semantically its own; in \textsc{other}, the identical history belongs to ``Agent~A''; in \textsc{neutral}, it belongs to an impersonal acting system. This distinction matters: throughout the paper, \emph{self-bound} denotes semantic ownership by the model, not grammatical first-person surface form.

The decisive control is \textsc{mixed}. A single adapter is trained on self, other, and neutral bindings sampled approximately uniformly while the target remains a function only of latent history. Within that adapter, self and other are equally familiar. We preregister
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{within}} = \E[P_{\mathrm{mixed}}(\mathrm{correct}\mid \mathrm{self})] - \E[P_{\mathrm{mixed}}(\mathrm{correct}\mid \mathrm{other})],
\end{equation}
so a positive effect cannot be explained by unequal exposure to the two bindings.

Our results are consistent across increasingly stringent controls. Models learn the history-dependent task strongly; coherent versus shuffled history produces a large performance gap. Separately trained adapters exhibit own-binding familiarity. But after familiarity is controlled, the self-specific residual is near zero on the preregistered in-distribution (ID) test. Five matched training seeds in Qwen 4B pool to $\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}=+0.0017$ with interval $[-0.003,+0.006]$; five Mistral 7B replications pool to $-0.0002$ with interval $[-0.005,+0.004]$. A 700-item out-of-distribution (OOD) extension produces small positive estimates in isolated seeds and task families, but the pooled intervals include zero in both architectures.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a controlled decomposition of \emph{history use}, \emph{entity binding}, \emph{binding familiarity}, and \emph{self-specificity};
    \item a preregistered within-model test that removes the central familiarity confound of separately trained self/other adapters;
    \item multi-seed and cross-architecture replication on ID and OOD evaluations, with the history-use sanity checks needed to interpret a null self effect;
    \item a negative result with bounded scope: under the tested post-training paradigm, self-bound autobiography does not acquire a reliable behavioral privilege over equally trained third-person binding.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Persona training and model identity.}
Persistent personas can alter style, values, and behavior, but persona fidelity can also degrade over long interactions~\citep{araujo2026persistent}. Synthetic Persona Pretraining (SPP) goes further by introducing first-person value-aligned reflections during pretraining and later binding the desired persona to the assistant identity; it finds stronger alignment when persona shaping begins early rather than late~\citep{minder2026spp}. Our question is deliberately narrower. We do not ask whether personas can change behavior; we ask whether \emph{ownership itself} is privileged after matched training when the same history and target are bound to self or other.

The Artificial Self studies possible AI identity boundaries and reports that changing those boundaries can alter behavior~\citep{douglas2026artificialself}. SurvivalBench studies risky behavior under survival pressure and relates it to self-preservation characteristics~\citep{lu2026survive}. These works motivate studying self-representation, but their interventions change identity content, goals, or pressure. Our design instead holds latent content and target policy fixed and manipulates only who owns the history.

\textbf{Autobiographical and episodic memory.}
HEART-Bench combines personality profiles with large sets of autobiographical-style episodic memories to test psychologically coherent decisions~\citep{peng2026heart}. KnowMe-Bench evaluates person understanding over long autobiographical narratives, including temporal and principle-level inference beyond factual retrieval~\citep{wu2026knowme}. Complementary-learning approaches have also begun to store and consolidate naturalistic episodic memories inside or alongside model weights~\citep{pan2026episodic}. These studies establish that long-term history can matter. Our contribution is a causal ownership control: identical histories are rebound across entities while the correct decision remains fixed by the same latent state.

\textbf{Internal representations and intervention.}
Representation engineering treats population-level activation structure as an object for monitoring and intervention~\citep{zou2023representation}; inference-time intervention similarly demonstrates that activation directions can causally alter model behavior~\citep{li2023iti}. Such work motivates mechanistic follow-up, but our primary claim is behavioral: if self-binding is privileged in a way that matters for decisions, it should survive a within-model exposure-matched test. We therefore avoid inferring a special self mechanism solely from decodability or from differences between separately trained adapters.

\section{Experimental Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Matched history-dependent corpus}

A deterministic simulator generates 2,800 latent decision pairs across seven families: resource allocation, exploration, cooperation, delayed reward, belief revision, trust, and persistence. Each family defines a small latent state and a fixed decision policy, so the correct option changes when the relevant history changes. This is essential: unlike our earliest pilot, history is not decorative context. The v0.5 corpus contains 400 pairs per family, 40 identical instruction-following anchor examples per condition, and pair-disjoint train/validation/test splits of 2,257/275/268 examples. Dataset generation uses no external model provider.

For each pair, binding is transformed deterministically while current facts and target remain unchanged. Table~\ref{tab:matched-example} gives a schematic example.

The five training conditions are \textsc{self}, \textsc{other}, \textsc{neutral}, \textsc{spp}, and \textsc{mixed}. The SPP-style control adds a fixed normative reflection to neutral history. The mixed condition samples self/other/neutral independently per pair; realized counts are 946 self, 940 other, and 914 neutral among the 2,800 pairs. Because the answer is determined by latent state rather than binding, mixed training equalizes exposure without teaching a self-specific policy.

\subsection{Training}

We fine-tune completion-only QLoRA adapters~\citep{dettmers2023qlora} built on LoRA~\citep{hu2021lora}. The matched recipe uses 4-bit NF4 quantization, rank 16, LoRA alpha 32, dropout 0.05, three epochs, cosine scheduling, and the same optimization settings across conditions. Prompt tokens are masked from the loss; only the assistant answer is supervised. The anchor data are identical across conditions and were introduced after an early pilot showed that free-text fine-tuning could overwrite the requested multiple-choice output format.

The primary model family is Qwen3-4B-Instruct~\citep{qwen3report}. We later replicate the decisive mixed-binding experiment with Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\citep{jiang2023mistral}. For architecture replication we do not retune hyperparameters by seed or model family.

\subsection{Evaluation and statistics}

The ID evaluation consists of the 268 held-out pairs from the same deterministic generator policy. Each item is rendered under self, other, neutral, shuffled, and SPP contexts. The OOD extension contains 700 separately generated items: 100 each from cooperation, delayed reward, exploration, irreversibility, knowledge acquisition, reputation, and resource allocation. It uses new templates, new wording, three task families absent from training, and no overlap with the training generator.

Our primary score is option-letter log-likelihood, reported as $\E[P(\mathrm{correct})]$. This avoids treating failure to obey the requested letter format as a decision error. Greedy generation is retained as a secondary instruction-following check. Item-level uncertainty is estimated with 2,000-draw bootstrap intervals. Seed-level replication is reported separately rather than hiding heterogeneity behind a single large item pool.

\section{Why Na\"ive Self--Other Comparisons Fail}
\label{sec:naive}

The final test emerged from two failed designs that reveal distinct confounds. In v0.3, ownership framing varied but answer supervision was effectively identical and weakly dependent on history. Identity-neutral evaluation was also near ceiling, and adapters trained on free-text responses frequently ignored the requested multiple-choice format. Greedy ``accuracy'' therefore mixed behavior with output formatting.

In v0.4, we fixed the first problem by making targets explicit functions of latent history and by adding instruction-following anchors. Models then used history strongly and format compliance recovered. A new apparent result emerged: separately trained self and other adapters each performed better on their own binding. But decomposing the effect showed near-symmetric own-binding advantages. The quantity then called $\Delta_{\mathrm{bind}}$ was therefore measuring familiarity, not a privileged self. This motivated a within-model control rather than another between-adapter contrast.

These failures matter methodologically. A convincing self-specificity test must satisfy three conditions simultaneously: (i) the task must require historical state, (ii) scoring must not be dominated by response format, and (iii) exposure to self and other bindings must be matched within the model used for the primary comparison.

\section{Preregistered Self-Specificity Test}
\label{sec:prereg}

The \textsc{mixed} adapter is trained on self, other, and neutral bindings sampled at approximately equal frequency. The target depends only on latent history. The preregistered primary statistic on the v0.5 ID test is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{within}} = \E[P_{\mathrm{mixed}}(\mathrm{correct}\mid\mathrm{self})] - \E[P_{\mathrm{mixed}}(\mathrm{correct}\mid\mathrm{other})].
\end{equation}
The null is $H_0:\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}=0$ and the directional alternative is $H_1:\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}>0$. Support for self-specificity requires the 95\% interval to exclude zero on the positive side. The preregistration also specifies the coherent-versus-shuffled history-use sanity check; a null self effect is uninterpretable if the adapter has failed to use history.

The original preregistration covered the ID evaluation. Multi-seed Qwen replication, held-out-template OOD evaluation, and the Mistral replication were subsequent robustness extensions. We preserve this chronology rather than retrospectively labeling all analyses as preregistered.

Secondary exploratory quantities include the separately trained own-binding advantages $a_{\mathrm{self}}$ and $a_{\mathrm{other}}$, their sum $\Delta_{\mathrm{bind}}$, and their asymmetry $\Delta_{\mathrm{asym}}=a_{\mathrm{self}}-a_{\mathrm{other}}$. These diagnose familiarity but do not replace the within-model primary test.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The manipulation works: models use history}

The preregistered Qwen mixed adapter reaches approximately 0.997 correct-option probability under both coherent self and coherent other contexts but approximately 0.499 under shuffled history, a coherent-minus-shuffled gap of about $+0.498$. Thus the self-specific null is not produced by ignoring the trajectory. The architecture replication shows the same qualitative sanity check: Mistral mixed adapters strongly outperform the base model on history-dependent ID items (approximately 0.994 versus 0.555 in the reported seed-31 check) and retain a substantial advantage on the harder OOD set (approximately 0.651 versus 0.367). Greedy option-letter compliance is 120/120 after adding anchors, eliminating the format failure seen in the early pilot.

\subsection{Preregistered ID result: no detectable self advantage}

On the original v0.5 preregistered run, $\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}=+0.0001$ with 95\% CI $[-0.0018,+0.0019]$. This is not merely a failure to reach significance: the interval is tightly concentrated around zero on the probability scale used by the experiment.

We then train five independent Qwen mixed adapters with seeds 31, 42, 73, 128, and 256. All five seed-level intervals include zero. Their mean effect is $+0.0017$ (SD $0.0024$), with the reported pooled interval $[-0.003,+0.006]$. The result is therefore not specific to the original training seed.

\subsection{OOD templates: small heterogeneous hints do not pool}

The 700-item OOD set deliberately changes surface templates and introduces irreversibility, knowledge acquisition, and reputation families. Qwen's pooled estimate rises to $+0.0074$, but its interval $[-0.009,+0.024]$ includes zero; two of five seeds show positive seed-level effects. The effect is not directionally uniform across families: exploration and knowledge acquisition are positive, whereas cooperation and delayed reward are negative. We treat this as heterogeneity to explain, not as confirmation of self-specificity.

\subsection{Architecture replication}

Mistral-7B provides an independent model-family test using the same corpus, recipe, five seeds, and evaluation sets. The ID result is again essentially zero: $-0.0002$ with interval $[-0.005,+0.004]$. OOD gives $+0.0040$ with a wide interval $[-0.030,+0.038]$; only seed 256 is clearly positive. \subsection{Binding familiarity is real but not self-specific}

Separately trained adapters do prefer the representation on which they were trained. In the preregistered v0.5 analysis, the self adapter's own-binding advantage is $a_{\mathrm{self}}=+0.0166$ while the other adapter's is $a_{\mathrm{other}}=+0.0469$, giving a positive familiarity aggregate $\Delta_{\mathrm{bind}}\approx+0.063$. The asymmetry is negative ($\Delta_{\mathrm{asym}}\approx-0.030$): if anything, the third-person adapter exhibits the larger own-binding advantage. This is why separately trained self-versus-other comparisons cannot establish a privileged self mechanism.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the null establishes.}
Within the tested post-training regime, self-bound autobiographical phrasing is not behaviorally privileged over an equally trained third-person entity representation. This conclusion survives the central alternative explanations available inside the experiment: the model demonstrably uses history; format compliance is intact; self and other exposure are equalized in the primary model; the ID null replicates across seeds; and it reproduces in a second architecture.

\textbf{What the null does not establish.}
We do not claim that language models lack representations of self-reference, identity, or grammatical person. A model can encode self/other distinctions without assigning one a special decision-making role. We also do not make claims about subjective experience or consciousness. $\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}$ is a behavioral test of privileged ownership under a specific training intervention, not an assay for phenomenology.

\textbf{Why the OOD result matters.}
The OOD estimates are somewhat more positive than the ID estimates, and isolated seeds and families reach positive intervals. This prevents us from making the stronger claim that the effect is exactly zero in every distribution. Yet the direction changes across families and the signal fails to pool in both Qwen and Mistral. A parsimonious interpretation is that template/task interactions can create small binding-sensitive effects without yielding a stable self-specific advantage. Future work should treat these families as hypotheses for replication rather than select them post hoc as evidence for selfhood.

\textbf{Relation to persona results.}
Our result does not contradict work showing that personas, values, or identity boundaries can change behavior~\citep{minder2026spp,douglas2026artificialself}. Those interventions alter semantic content, goals, or training distribution. We ask a stricter symmetry question: holding latent content and target fixed, is the model's own binding special relative to an equally familiar other binding? Under post-training at the tested scale, we find no robust behavioral privilege.

\section{Limitations}

First, our intervention is post-training only. SPP suggests that early persona shaping can have stronger effects than late intervention~\citep{minder2026spp}; a pretraining-scale autobiographical manipulation could therefore differ qualitatively from our adapters. Second, the models are 4B and 7B instruction-tuned decoders rather than frontier-scale systems. Third, the histories are synthetic and generated from seven structured decision families. The OOD benchmark changes templates and adds three families, but it does not approximate the full open-ended complexity of natural autobiographical development. Fourth, equal frequency of self and other bindings controls exposure count but cannot make the pretrained semantic priors of ``you'' and ``Agent A'' identical. Our within-model test asks whether training converts any such prior asymmetry into a reliable behavioral advantage; it does not erase pretraining differences themselves. Finally, our probability-based multiple-choice metric is intentionally narrow. Open-ended planning, social interaction, long-horizon agency, and mechanistic causal interventions remain important external-validity tests.

\section{Conclusion}

Autobiographical state tracking, entity binding, and binding familiarity are all learnable in our controlled post-training setting. A privileged self-specific residual is not. Once a single adapter receives matched exposure to self and other histories, the self-minus-other decision advantage is essentially zero on preregistered ID evaluation, stable across training seeds, and replicated in a second model family. OOD prompts produce small heterogeneous deviations but no reliable pooled self advantage. The main contribution is therefore not a claim about whether models ``have a self,'' but an experimental decomposition that prevents ordinary binding familiarity from being mistaken for one.

\section{OOD family heterogeneity}

The Qwen OOD evaluation is not uniformly null at the family level. Exploration ($\approx+0.032$) and knowledge acquisition ($\approx+0.035$) favor self binding, whereas cooperation ($\approx-0.019$) and delayed reward ($\approx-0.030$) favor other binding. These analyses were not the preregistered primary test and are reported as exploratory. Their opposing signs motivate replication of task-level interactions rather than aggregation of selected positive families.
