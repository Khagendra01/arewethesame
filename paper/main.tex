\begin{abstract}
We test whether assigning the same historical record to a language model's own identity changes its subsequent forced-choice behavior after matched history-dependent post-training. Separately trained SELF and OTHER adapters show own-binding advantages, motivating a mixed-binding control that gives a single model approximately balanced exposure to self, other, and neutral histories. On the original preregistered in-distribution evaluation, the mixed adapters show no detected SELF advantage in mean normalized designated-option score across five Qwen 4B and five Mistral 7B training seeds. We then preregister a harder 840-item identity-controlled evaluation: SELF and OTHER share byte-identical history bodies, differ only in which arbitrary alias is assigned as the assistant, and each item contains two coherent counterfactual histories that imply different simulator-policy actions. The primary statistic is an ownership-by-counterfactual-history log-odds interaction, $\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}$. Qwen is positive ($+0.208$, 95\% CI $[+0.119,+0.284]$), whereas Mistral is smaller and inconclusive ($+0.074$, $[-0.013,+0.162]$); the preregistered criterion for a robust effect in both model families is therefore not met. Separately, the mean normalized designated-policy option score satisfies the prospectively specified $\pm0.01$ equivalence criterion in both families. These results distinguish binding familiarity, average policy agreement, and counterfactual log-odds sensitivity. The primary interaction is real at the behavioral-score level but does not by itself identify a unique internal evidence-weighting mechanism.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models increasingly operate with persistent personas, memories, and long-lived agent identities. Recent work shows that persona information can be installed through prompting, post-training, or pretraining, and that long-term history can shape later behavior~\citep{minder2026spp,araujo2026persistent,peng2026heart,wu2026knowme,pan2026episodic}. A separate literature studies self-recognition, self-preference, assigned identity, and self-preservation~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition,wataoka2024selfpreference,chen2025beyond,lehr2025extreme,douglas2026artificialself,lu2026survive}. We study a narrower causal question: after a model is trained to use historical state, does assigning that state to SELF change how it affects subsequent choices relative to an equally familiar OTHER?

This question is easy to test incorrectly. A SELF-only adapter can prefer SELF phrasing simply because that phrasing is familiar; an OTHER-only adapter can show the symmetric effect. Self-preference work has identified analogous confounds from familiarity, output quality, and assigned identity~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition,wataoka2024selfpreference,chen2025beyond,lehr2025extreme,spiliopoulou2025playfavorites}. Our contribution is specific to \emph{history ownership}: latent task state and designated policy are controlled while ownership binding is manipulated.

We proceed in stages. A deterministic simulator generates history-dependent tasks. A \textsc{mixed} adapter is then trained on SELF, OTHER, and neutral bindings at approximately balanced frequency, so the main self--other contrast occurs within the same trained model. The original v0.5 preregistration measures the difference in mean normalized designated-option score,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}
=
\E[s_{\mathrm{mixed}}(y^*\mid\mathrm{SELF})]
-
\E[s_{\mathrm{mixed}}(y^*\mid\mathrm{OTHER})],
\end{equation}
where $s(y^*)$ is the normalized forced-choice score assigned to the simulator-designated option. v0.5 yields no detected positive effect on held-out in-distribution (ID) items across Qwen and Mistral replications, but the assay is near ceiling and changes surface form from ``you'' to a named third-person entity.

We therefore preregister v0.6 before evaluating any v0.6 model outputs. The harder benchmark has 840 items. SELF and OTHER use byte-identical historical bodies; only a short identity header determines whether the historical owner alias denotes the assistant or another agent. Each item contains two coherent counterfactual histories, $H_0$ and $H_1$, whose simulator policies designate different actions. This supports a direct interaction:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}
=
\E[S_{\mathrm{SELF}}-S_{\mathrm{OTHER}}],
\end{equation}
where $S_b$ is the change in relative log-odds between the two history-contingent actions when the history changes. Qwen satisfies the preregistered positive-effect criterion; Mistral does not. We therefore report evidence for an ownership-dependent counterfactual log-odds interaction in Qwen and an inconclusive replication in Mistral, not a universal self mechanism or a statistically established difference between the two model families.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a matched, within-model design that separates ordinary binding familiarity from ownership-dependent behavior;
    \item two preregistered stages, including a harder identity-controlled benchmark with paired counterfactual histories and crossed seed--item uncertainty;
    \item evidence that mean designated-policy score can be practically equivalent between SELF and OTHER even when a preregistered log-odds interaction is positive in one model family; and
    \item an explicit account of what the interaction does \emph{not} identify: generic confidence scaling, discourse salience, and the stipulated simulator policy remain alternative explanations unless separately controlled.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Self-preference, self-recognition, and assigned identity.}
Panickssery et al.~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition} show that LLM evaluators can recognize and favor their own generations. Wataoka et al.~\citep{wataoka2024selfpreference} connect apparent self-preference to familiarity measured through perplexity. Chen et al.~\citep{chen2025beyond} show that naive self-preference scores can mix preference with response quality. Spiliopoulou et al.~\citep{spiliopoulou2025playfavorites} formalize assumptions under which self-bias can be identified separately from underlying output quality. Most directly for identity assignment, Lehr et al.~\citep{lehr2025extreme} find that self-favoring behavior follows experimentally assigned identities. These papers establish that identity effects can be real while naive measurements can be confounded. Our estimand is different: we test an ownership-by-counterfactual-history interaction after matched task training.

\textbf{Persona and memory training.}
Synthetic Persona Pretraining introduces persona reflections during pretraining and later binds the persona to the assistant identity~\citep{minder2026spp}; persistent-persona work studies stability over long interactions~\citep{araujo2026persistent}. HEART-Bench and KnowMe-Bench evaluate decisions or person understanding from autobiographical-style histories~\citep{peng2026heart,wu2026knowme}, and continual-memory work studies episodic storage and consolidation~\citep{pan2026episodic}. Our histories are substantially narrower. A task policy is learned during post-training, while held-out historical records are supplied in the evaluation prompt; we therefore avoid claiming that v0.5/v0.6 demonstrates autonomous retrieval of accumulated autobiographical memory.

\textbf{Identity and behavior.}
The Artificial Self and survival-pressure work study identity boundaries and self-preservation-related behavior~\citep{douglas2026artificialself,lu2026survive}. Our experiment is intentionally more constrained: the same task structure and history body are compared under different ownership assignments.

\textbf{Representation and intervention.}
Representation engineering and inference-time intervention show that activation-space manipulations can alter behavior~\citep{zou2023representation,li2023iti}. We make no mechanistic representation claim here. $\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}$ is an output-level interaction; an internal mechanism would require additional causal or representational evidence.

\section{Experimental Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Training corpus and ownership conditions}

A deterministic simulator generates 2,800 latent decision pairs across seven families: resource allocation, exploration, cooperation, delayed reward, belief revision, trust, and persistence. Each family implements a stipulated policy that maps historical state to one of three options. These policies provide controlled labels for testing acquisition and use of state; they are not asserted to be universally normative decisions.

The v0.5 corpus contains 400 pairs per family, 40 identical instruction-following anchor examples per condition, and pair-disjoint train/validation/test splits of 2,257/275/268 pairs. For each latent pair, ownership is changed while the task state and designated option are held fixed. The five conditions are \textsc{self}, \textsc{other}, \textsc{neutral}, \textsc{mixed}, and an \textsc{spp-inspired} reflection control. The mixed condition assigns one of self/other/neutral to each pair by a fixed deterministic procedure; aggregate counts are 946/940/914 over the 2,800 pairs. This approximately balances task-training exposure, but does not equalize pretrained familiarity or make every latent training item counterfactually appear under all bindings.

We train completion-only QLoRA adapters~\citep{dettmers2023qlora,hu2021lora} with 4-bit NF4 quantization, rank 16, LoRA alpha 32, dropout 0.05, three epochs, and matched optimization settings. Prompt tokens are masked from the loss. The two model families are Qwen3-4B-Instruct~\citep{qwen3report} and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\citep{jiang2023mistral}. Mixed adapters use training seeds $\{31,42,73,128,256\}$ in each family; identical numeric seed labels across families do not imply paired stochastic runs.

\subsection{v0.5 evaluation}

The ID set contains 268 held-out pairs from the same generator policy. A later OOD extension contains 700 items with new templates and three task families absent from training. v0.5 uses a normalized forced-choice option score and reports its SELF-minus-OTHER mean. Because v0.5 specified a directional positive-effect test but no smallest effect of interest, intervals spanning zero are interpreted as non-detection rather than equivalence.

\subsection{v0.6 identity-controlled benchmark}

v0.6 was frozen before model evaluation. It contains 840 latent items, 120 per family. Each item has two coherent histories $H_0,H_1$ placed near a simulator-policy boundary, and the designated action differs between histories. Histories require derived quantities such as counts, subtraction, or ratios and contain irrelevant administrative distractors.

The history body is byte-identical across SELF and OTHER. If the historical owner is \texttt{Agent K}, the identity header can state either
\begin{quote}\small
You are Agent K. Agent M is a different agent.
\end{quote}
or
\begin{quote}\small
You are Agent M. Agent K is a different agent.
\end{quote}
followed by the same historical sentence referring to Agent K. Alias pairs are deterministically sampled from a fixed pool and are exactly matched within each SELF--OTHER item comparison; they are \emph{not} exactly counterbalanced in aggregate. The current header also places the historical owner first under SELF and second under OTHER, leaving discourse position/salience as a remaining alternative explanation.

\subsection{v0.6 primary statistic}

Let $c_0$ and $c_1$ be the actions designated by the simulator policy under $H_0$ and $H_1$. For binding $b$,
\begin{equation}
S_b=
\left[\log p(c_0\mid H_0,b)-\log p(c_1\mid H_0,b)\right]
-
\left[\log p(c_0\mid H_1,b)-\log p(c_1\mid H_1,b)\right].
\end{equation}
The preregistered primary statistic is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}=\E[S_{\mathrm{SELF}}-S_{\mathrm{OTHER}}].
\end{equation}
It is directly interpretable as an ownership-by-counterfactual-history interaction in output log-odds. We use ``history sensitivity'' as shorthand for this operational quantity. Importantly, it does not uniquely identify internal reliance on historical evidence: for example, an ownership-dependent global scaling of option logits can increase $S_b$ without changing argmax choices or evidence selection.

The preregistration requires the lower endpoint of a two-sided 95\% hierarchical interval to exceed zero for a positive effect, and requires this criterion in both model families for a robust cross-family claim.

\subsection{Forced-choice scoring and uncertainty}

For v0.6, each option letter is scored at the next-token position. For each of A/B/C, the evaluator considers eligible single-token bare-letter and leading-space realizations and takes the maximum next-token log-probability among them; the resulting three scores are normalized across A/B/C. Thus the reported quantity is a \emph{normalized forced-choice score}, not the unconditional probability that ordinary free generation emits the correct answer. Pairwise log-odds are unaffected by the A/B/C normalization, although the max-over-token-realizations convention is itself a scoring choice.

Secondary metrics are SELF-minus-OTHER mean normalized designated-policy score ($\Delta_{\mathrm{prob}}$ in code), correct-vs-best-incorrect log margin, and hard forced-choice accuracy. v0.6 prospectively specifies practical equivalence for the mean normalized designated-policy score at $\pm0.01$. The claim applies only to this mean score and does not imply equivalence of individual choices, families, hard accuracy, or output distributions.

Pooled intervals use a crossed hierarchical bootstrap with 5,000 draws: training seeds and latent item IDs are resampled while all SELF/OTHER and $H_0/H_1$ variants remain paired. Synthetic SELF-only score injections are retained as an algebraic/software sanity check. Because adding bias $\beta$ toward the designated action in each SELF history mechanically shifts $\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}$ by $2\beta$, this check does not establish identity understanding, model-level assay power under a null system, or separation from generic confidence scaling.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{v0.5: history use is strong; a positive SELF score advantage is not detected}

Qwen's v0.5 mixed adapter assigns approximately 0.997 normalized designated-option score under coherent SELF and OTHER contexts but about 0.499 under shuffled history, showing that the learned task depends strongly on the supplied history. The original preregistered Qwen estimate is $\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}=+0.0001$ with 95\% CI $[-0.0018,+0.0019]$. Across five Qwen seeds, the ID mean is $+0.0017$ with pooled interval $[-0.003,+0.006]$; five Mistral seeds give $-0.0002$ $[-0.005,+0.004]$. Because no equivalence margin was preregistered for v0.5, these are non-detections, not evidence of exact equality.

The OOD extension is less stable: Qwen pools to $+0.0074$ $[-0.009,+0.024]$ and Mistral to $+0.0040$ $[-0.030,+0.038]$, with sign changes across families and isolated positive seeds. Separately trained adapters also show own-binding familiarity, motivating the within-model mixed design rather than between-adapter inference.

\subsection{v0.6 reduces the earlier saturation and lexical-history confounds}

On v0.6, mixed-adapter hard forced-choice accuracy is about 0.65, substantially below the near-ceiling v0.5 ID performance. This reduces the earlier saturation concern but does not by itself prove that all per-prediction score distributions are unsaturated. The history body is held fixed across ownership conditions, removing the direct ``you'' versus ``Agent A'' difference inside the historical record; the header-order/salience confound remains.

The earlier v0.5 Qwen base-model SELF advantage is also much smaller under the v0.6 assigned-alias realization: the base mean normalized score difference is about $+0.0025$, while the base margin difference is negative. Because task difficulty and identity wording both changed between v0.5 and v0.6, this comparison is descriptive and does not isolate which change caused the earlier asymmetry to disappear.

\subsection{Mean normalized designated-policy score meets the prospective equivalence criterion}

For Qwen mixed adapters,
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{prob}}=+0.0003,\qquad 95\%~\mathrm{CI}=[-0.006,+0.006].
\]
For Mistral,
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{prob}}=+0.0008,\qquad 95\%~\mathrm{CI}=[-0.003,+0.005].
\]
Both intervals lie within the prospectively specified $\pm0.01$ region. We therefore claim practical equivalence only for the \emph{mean normalized designated-policy option score} under this benchmark and scoring convention. We do not infer equivalence of hard accuracy, individual decisions, task families, or confidence distributions from this result.

\subsection{The preregistered log-odds interaction is positive in Qwen and inconclusive in Mistral}

Qwen mixed adapters show
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}=+0.208,
\qquad 95\%~\mathrm{CI}=[+0.119,+0.284],
\]
which meets the preregistered positive-effect criterion. Mistral mixed adapters show
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}=+0.074,
\qquad 95\%~\mathrm{CI}=[-0.013,+0.162],
\]
which does not. The preregistered requirement for a robust positive result in both model families is therefore not met.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the primary result establishes.}
Under the frozen v0.6 score, changing the supplied counterfactual history changes relative action log-odds more under SELF than OTHER in Qwen. This is an ownership-dependent behavioral-score interaction. It is stronger than a simple SELF-minus-OTHER average because history itself changes within each ownership condition.

\textbf{What it does not establish.}
The interaction does not uniquely identify increased internal reliance on historical evidence. A generic ownership-dependent temperature or confidence effect could amplify the same history-driven preference differences while leaving the underlying argmax rule unchanged. Likewise, the current identity header bundles ownership with antecedent order and discourse salience. These alternatives require targeted controls rather than stronger prose.

\textbf{Why the mean-score equivalence remains useful.}
The prospectively specified equivalence result answers a narrower question: SELF assignment does not shift the \emph{mean normalized designated-policy option score} by more than the specified one-point region in either family. That finding can coexist with changes in individual decisions, confidence, or counterfactual sensitivity. Reporting both quantities prevents a null average from being mistaken for identical behavior.

\textbf{Labels measure policy agreement, not normative quality.}
The simulator policies are engineered thresholds. For example, v0.6 belief revision includes a high-bias branch whose designated action is to adopt the opposite hypothesis even though the prompt does not specify enough evidence to make that action normatively entailed. We therefore interpret the task as testing acquisition and use of a stipulated policy. The experiment does not establish that SELF makes decisions objectively better in an external environment.

\textbf{Relation to prior self-bias work.}
Our novelty does not rest on the generic claims that assigned identity matters or that self-preference can be confounded; both are established in prior work~\citep{wataoka2024selfpreference,lehr2025extreme,spiliopoulou2025playfavorites}. The narrower contribution is a matched ownership-by-history experiment after approximately balanced task training, together with paired counterfactual histories that make history sensitivity directly measurable.

\section{Limitations}

First, v0.6 leaves a header-order and discourse-salience confound: the historical owner appears first under SELF and second under OTHER. Second, $\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}$ can respond to generic logit scaling as well as history-specific evidence use. Third, the simulator labels are stipulated policies rather than general normative truths. Fourth, the normalized forced-choice score is not unconditional free-generation probability, and the v0.6 max-over-token-realizations convention is one of several reasonable scoring choices. Fifth, the mixed training assignment is approximately balanced rather than fully counterfactual at the training-item level, and all five optimizer seeds reuse the same corpus and binding assignment. Sixth, the two checkpoint families differ in size, pretraining, instruction tuning, and tokenization; even a significant direct Qwen--Mistral interaction would not isolate architecture as the cause. Finally, the experiments use synthetic, short histories supplied in the prompt rather than naturalistic lifelong memory.

\section{Ethics and Broader Impact}

Claims about model ``selfhood'' can be overinterpreted. We therefore separate operational behavioral quantities from phenomenological claims. Evidence of an ownership-dependent log-odds interaction does not establish consciousness, subjective selfhood, moral patienthood, or a unified self-model. Conversely, identity-conditioned behavior in long-lived agents may still matter for safety even when its mechanism is ordinary confidence, salience, or learned reference resolution.

\section{Conclusion}

Matched training and within-model controls show why a SELF-preferring model is not automatically evidence of a privileged self representation: binding familiarity alone can generate own-binding advantages. On the harder assigned-identity benchmark, SELF and OTHER satisfy a prespecified practical-equivalence criterion for the mean normalized designated-policy option score in both Qwen and Mistral. The preregistered ownership-by-counterfactual-history log-odds interaction is nevertheless positive in Qwen and inconclusive in Mistral. The strongest present conclusion is therefore deliberately operational: assigned SELF identity can modify counterfactual action-score sensitivity in at least one tested checkpoint family, without a corresponding mean policy-score advantage. Determining whether this reflects history-specific evidence use, generic confidence amplification, or prompt salience requires additional controls.

\section{Interpretation of the v0.6 injected-score control}

The v0.6 analysis adds bias $\beta$ toward the simulator-designated option in each SELF history. Because the designated option changes across the paired histories, the induced shift in the primary interaction is algebraically $2\beta$. We therefore retain this control as a software and scoring-pipeline sanity check, not as a behavioral positive control or as evidence that the model understands identity.

\section{Additional planned evidence checks}

Before the final supplementary artifact is frozen, the raw v0.6 outputs are checked for exactly 840 common item IDs and four SELF/OTHER $\times$ history variants per item. Reviewer-targeted reanalysis reports the primary base-versus-mixed contrast, a direct Qwen--Mistral checkpoint-family interaction with independent seed resampling, leave-one-seed-out estimates, family heterogeneity, ownership-induced argmax switch rates, absolute score changes, and per-variant score/entropy diagnostics. These analyses do not change the preregistered v0.6 primary estimand.
