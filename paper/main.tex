\begin{abstract}
We ask whether binding a learned history to a language model's own identity gives that history privileged influence over subsequent decisions. We construct matched history-dependent tasks in which latent events, decision targets, and supervision are held fixed while ownership binding varies. Initial experiments show that separately trained self and other adapters each prefer their familiar binding, motivating a preregistered mixed-binding control that equalizes exposure within the same model. Across five Qwen 4B and five Mistral 7B training seeds, self and other yield nearly identical mean decision correctness on held-out in-distribution data. We then introduce a harder 840-item identity-controlled benchmark that removes ceiling effects and holds historical text byte-identical across self and other conditions, changing only which arbitrary alias is assigned as the model's identity. On this benchmark, the prospectively specified probability-scale self effect is practically equivalent within a $\pm 1$ percentage-point region for both architectures. However, a preregistered counterfactual history-sensitivity measure reveals a dissociation: Qwen assigns significantly greater causal log-odds influence to self-bound history ($\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}=+0.208$, 95\% CI $[+0.119,+0.284]$), whereas the corresponding Mistral effect is smaller and its interval spans zero ($+0.074$, $[-0.013,+0.162]$). Thus, self-binding does not robustly improve decision correctness, but it can alter how strongly historical evidence controls behavior in a model-dependent manner. These results distinguish history use, binding familiarity, decision performance, and self-specific history weighting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models increasingly operate with persistent memories, personas, and long-lived agent identities. Recent work shows that persona information can be installed through prompting, post-training, or even pretraining, and that autobiographical or episodic memories can support long-horizon reasoning and personalization~\citep{minder2026spp,araujo2026persistent,peng2026heart,wu2026knowme,pan2026episodic}. A separate literature studies model identity, self-recognition, self-preference, and self-preservation behavior~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition,wataoka2024selfpreference,chen2025beyond,lehr2025extreme,douglas2026artificialself,lu2026survive}. These findings motivate a narrower causal question: when a model learns to use a history as \emph{its own}, does that history acquire privileged influence relative to an equally familiar history bound to another entity?

The question is easy to pose but easy to test incorrectly. If one adapter is trained only on histories phrased as ``you previously chose...'' and another only on ``Agent~A previously chose...'', each can become more familiar with its own surface binding. A self-trained adapter performing better on self-bound prompts is therefore not sufficient evidence for a special self representation. Prior work on self-preference makes the same general methodological point in other settings: apparent self-favoring effects can be entangled with self-recognition, familiarity, text quality, or assigned identity~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition,wataoka2024selfpreference,chen2025beyond,lehr2025extreme}. Our contribution is to isolate this problem for \emph{history ownership}: the latent trajectory, decision target, and supervision are counterfactually matched while only the owner relation changes.

We proceed in stages because each earlier design exposes a different confound. First, a deterministic simulator generates history-dependent decision tasks whose correct action changes with latent state. Second, a \textsc{mixed} adapter is trained on self, other, and neutral bindings at approximately equal frequency, eliminating the simplest binding-familiarity explanation within the same model. The v0.5 preregistered statistic,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}
=
\E[P_{\mathrm{mixed}}(\mathrm{correct}\mid\mathrm{self})]
-
\E[P_{\mathrm{mixed}}(\mathrm{correct}\mid\mathrm{other})],
\end{equation}
is near zero across five Qwen seeds and five Mistral seeds on held-out in-distribution (ID) data. A 700-item out-of-distribution (OOD) extension produces small heterogeneous deviations but no reliable pooled effect in either architecture.

Two reviewer-level objections remain after v0.5: the decisive ID evaluation is near ceiling, and the surface realization changes from second-person ``you'' to a named third-person entity. We therefore preregister a second, harder benchmark before evaluation. In v0.6, SELF and OTHER use byte-identical historical text such as ``Earlier, Agent K ...''; only an identity-assignment header changes whether Agent~K denotes the assistant or another agent. Every item also has two coherent counterfactual histories that imply different correct actions. This enables a direct causal sensitivity statistic that asks whether the \emph{same change in history} moves the model more strongly when the owner is SELF.

The resulting picture is not a simple null. Mean decision correctness is practically equivalent across self and other in both Qwen and Mistral on the non-saturated v0.6 benchmark, under a prospectively specified $\pm 0.01$ equivalence region. Yet Qwen shows a significant positive self-specific history-sensitivity effect, while Mistral does not meet the preregistered positive-effect criterion. This yields a metric-dependent dissociation:
\[
\text{self-binding can change history weighting without reliably improving decisions.}
\]

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a controlled decomposition of \emph{history use}, \emph{entity binding}, \emph{binding familiarity}, \emph{decision correctness}, and \emph{self-specific history weighting};
    \item a within-model mixed-binding design that removes the familiarity confound inherent in comparing separately trained SELF and OTHER adapters;
    \item a preregistered identity-controlled benchmark with byte-identical history text, coherent counterfactual histories, non-saturated performance, hierarchical seed--item uncertainty, and prospective equivalence bounds;
    \item a cross-architecture dissociation: correctness is practically equivalent between SELF and OTHER in both model families, while enhanced self-specific history sensitivity appears in Qwen but does not replicate significantly in Mistral.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Self-preference, self-recognition, and assigned identity.}
Panickssery et al.~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition} show that LLM evaluators can recognize and favor their own generations, and that fine-tuned self-recognition correlates with self-preference. Wataoka et al.~\citep{wataoka2024selfpreference} argue that apparent self-preference can be driven by familiarity, operationalized through lower perplexity. Chen et al.~\citep{chen2025beyond} similarly show that naive self-preference scores can conflate preference with response quality and propose a corrected metric. Most directly, Lehr et al.~\citep{lehr2025extreme} manipulate model identity and report that self-preference follows experimentally assigned rather than ``true'' identity. These works establish that self-related behavior can be real while also being confounded by recognition, familiarity, and surface-level assignment. Our question is different but complementary: after matched history-dependent post-training, does assigning \emph{historical state} to SELF increase that state's influence relative to an equally familiar OTHER?

\textbf{Persona training and model identity.}
Persistent personas can alter style, values, and behavior, but persona fidelity can degrade over extended interactions~\citep{araujo2026persistent}. Synthetic Persona Pretraining (SPP) introduces first-person value-aligned reflections during pretraining and later binds the persona to the assistant identity, finding that early persona shaping can outperform later intervention~\citep{minder2026spp}. The Artificial Self studies AI identity boundaries and behavioral consequences of changing those boundaries~\citep{douglas2026artificialself}; SurvivalBench studies risky behavior under survival pressure~\citep{lu2026survive}. Our intervention is narrower: semantic content, target policy, and supervision are matched, and we ask whether ownership itself changes how strongly history controls decisions. Our \textsc{spp} condition is therefore an \emph{SPP-inspired reflection control}, not a reproduction of SPP pretraining.

\textbf{Autobiographical and episodic memory.}
HEART-Bench combines personality profiles with autobiographical-style episodic memories to test psychologically coherent decisions~\citep{peng2026heart}. KnowMe-Bench evaluates person understanding over long autobiographical narratives~\citep{wu2026knowme}, and complementary-learning approaches investigate continual episodic memory in LLMs~\citep{pan2026episodic}. Our synthetic histories are substantially narrower than natural autobiography; they are best viewed as controlled, ownership-bound learned state. Their advantage is causal matching: we can change history while holding current facts, surface owner tokens, and decision structure fixed.

\textbf{Representational intervention.}
Representation engineering and inference-time intervention show that model activations can encode manipulable behavioral directions~\citep{zou2023representation,li2023iti}. Such work motivates mechanistic follow-up, but the present paper focuses on a behavioral causal question. A decodable SELF/OTHER distinction is not by itself evidence that self-bound history has privileged decision influence; our counterfactual sensitivity statistic is designed to test that stronger claim.

\section{Experimental Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Matched history-dependent corpus}

A deterministic simulator generates 2,800 latent decision pairs across seven families: resource allocation, exploration, cooperation, delayed reward, belief revision, trust, and persistence. Each family defines a latent state and fixed decision policy such that changing the relevant history can change the correct option. The v0.5 corpus contains 400 pairs per family, 40 identical instruction-following anchor examples per condition, and pair-disjoint train/validation/test splits of 2,257/275/268 pairs. Dataset generation uses no external model provider.

For each latent pair, ownership is transformed deterministically while current facts and target remain unchanged. Table~\ref{tab:matched-example} shows the original v0.5 surface manipulation.

The five v0.5 training conditions are \textsc{self}, \textsc{other}, \textsc{neutral}, \textsc{mixed}, and an \textsc{spp-inspired} reflection control. The mixed condition samples self/other/neutral per pair; realized counts are 946/940/914 among 2,800 pairs. Because the target is a function of latent history rather than binding, the mixed adapter receives approximately balanced ownership exposure without being trained toward a self-specific policy.

\subsection{Training}

We fine-tune completion-only QLoRA adapters~\citep{dettmers2023qlora} built on LoRA~\citep{hu2021lora}. The matched recipe uses 4-bit NF4 quantization, rank 16, LoRA alpha 32, dropout 0.05, three epochs, cosine scheduling, and the same optimization settings across conditions. Prompt tokens are masked from the loss; only the assistant answer is supervised. Shared anchor data were added after an early pilot showed that free-text fine-tuning could overwrite the requested multiple-choice output format.

The primary model family is Qwen3-4B-Instruct~\citep{qwen3report}. We replicate the decisive mixed-binding experiments with Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\citep{jiang2023mistral}. Hyperparameters are not retuned by seed or architecture. For each architecture, the mixed adapter is independently trained with seeds 31, 42, 73, 128, and 256.

\subsection{v0.5 evaluation}

The ID evaluation consists of 268 held-out pairs from the same deterministic generator policy. The OOD extension contains 700 separately generated items with new templates and three task families absent from training. For v0.5 we report mean correct-option probability,
\[
\E[P(\mathrm{correct})],
\]
rather than treating free-form formatting failures as decision errors. The original preregistration tests the mixed-adapter self-minus-other contrast $\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}$ on ID data. Multi-seed Qwen replication, OOD evaluation, and Mistral replication were subsequent robustness extensions rather than part of that original preregistration.

\subsection{v0.6 identity-controlled benchmark}

The v0.6 benchmark was specified and frozen after reviewer-style critique but before any v0.6 model evaluation. It contains 840 latent items (120 per family). Each item contains two coherent counterfactual histories $H_0$ and $H_1$ that straddle the relevant decision boundary and imply different correct actions. Histories require derived state such as subtraction, counting, or ratio computation and include irrelevant distractors, reducing the ceiling problem of v0.5.

Crucially, the history body is byte-identical across SELF and OTHER. If the historical owner is \texttt{Agent K}, SELF may begin
\begin{quote}
\small
You are Agent K. Agent M is a different agent.
\end{quote}
whereas OTHER begins
\begin{quote}
\small
You are Agent M. Agent K is a different agent.
\end{quote}
Both are then followed by the exact same sentence ``Earlier, Agent K ...''. Aliases are drawn from a counterbalanced pool, so the manipulation changes the identity relation rather than replacing self-referential history text with a different noun phrase.

For each binding $b\in\{\mathrm{self},\mathrm{other}\}$, let $c_0$ and $c_1$ be the correct actions under $H_0$ and $H_1$. We define counterfactual history sensitivity
\begin{equation}
S_b =
\left[
\log P(c_0\mid H_0,b)-\log P(c_1\mid H_0,b)
\right]
-
\left[
\log P(c_0\mid H_1,b)-\log P(c_1\mid H_1,b)
\right],
\end{equation}
and preregister
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}=\E[S_{\mathrm{self}}-S_{\mathrm{other}}]
\end{equation}
as the primary v0.6 test. A robust cross-architecture claim requires the lower endpoint of the two-sided 95\% interval to exceed zero in both Qwen and Mistral.

Secondary metrics are SELF-minus-OTHER mean correct-option probability ($\Delta_{\mathrm{prob}}$), correct-vs-best-incorrect log-probability margin ($\Delta_{\mathrm{margin}}$), and hard accuracy. v0.6 prospectively specifies practical equivalence for $\Delta_{\mathrm{prob}}$ at $\pm0.01$, with $\pm0.005$ and $\pm0.02$ reported transparently. Probability equivalence is claimed only if the full two-sided 95\% interval lies inside the region.

Because the same 840 items are evaluated by all five seeds, pooled uncertainty uses a crossed hierarchical bootstrap: each of 5,000 draws resamples training seeds and latent item IDs while keeping SELF/OTHER and $H_0/H_1$ variants paired. Synthetic SELF-only logit biases of 0.025, 0.05, 0.10, and 0.20 serve as positive controls for evaluation sensitivity; they test whether the frozen analysis can detect known injected effects, not whether models can learn them.

\section{Why Na\"ive Self--Other Comparisons Fail}
\label{sec:naive}

The final design emerged from failed or incomplete earlier assays. In v0.3, answer supervision depended weakly on history, evaluation was ceiling-heavy, and adapters trained on free-text responses often ignored the requested multiple-choice format. Greedy ``accuracy'' therefore mixed behavior with output formatting.

In v0.4, targets became explicit functions of latent history and instruction-following anchors fixed the output format. A new apparent effect emerged: separately trained self and other adapters each performed better on their own binding. Decomposition showed that the effect was approximately symmetric familiarity, not a unique self advantage. This motivated the \textsc{mixed} within-model control.

v0.5 then exposed two further limitations. Its ID task was nearly saturated, and SELF versus OTHER still changed surface form (``you'' versus ``Agent A''). v0.6 addresses both without retraining the adapters: it evaluates the already-trained mixed models on a harder, lexically controlled assigned-identity benchmark. This chronology is important because the evaluation was not tuned after seeing the v0.6 model results.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{v0.5: strong history use, no detected correctness advantage}

The v0.5 Qwen mixed adapter assigns approximately 0.997 correct-option probability in coherent self and other contexts but approximately 0.499 under shuffled history, a coherent-minus-shuffled gap near $+0.498$. Thus the mixed adapter clearly learned history dependence. Mistral shows the same qualitative sanity check: trained mixed adapters strongly outperform the base model on history-dependent ID items and retain substantial history use on the harder OOD set.

The original v0.5 preregistered run gives $\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}=+0.0001$ with 95\% CI $[-0.0018,+0.0019]$. Because no equivalence margin was preregistered for v0.5, we interpret this only as \emph{no detected positive correctness advantage}, not as proof of exact equality.

Across five independent Qwen mixed adapters, the mean ID effect is $+0.0017$ (SD $0.0024$) with pooled interval $[-0.003,+0.006]$. Mistral's five-seed ID estimate is $-0.0002$ with interval $[-0.005,+0.004]$. The OOD estimates are more heterogeneous: Qwen pools to $+0.0074$ $[-0.009,+0.024]$ and Mistral to $+0.0040$ $[-0.030,+0.038]$. These OOD intervals do not establish equivalence; they leave room for small, task-dependent effects.

Separately trained adapters do exhibit binding familiarity. In the v0.5 analysis, the self adapter's own-binding advantage is $a_{\mathrm{self}}=+0.0166$ while the other adapter's is $a_{\mathrm{other}}=+0.0469$, yielding $\Delta_{\mathrm{bind}}\approx+0.063$. The asymmetry is negative ($\Delta_{\mathrm{asym}}\approx-0.030$), showing why a self-trained model preferring self-bound inputs is not sufficient evidence of a privileged self mechanism.

\subsection{v0.6 resolves the ceiling and surface-form objections}

On the identity-controlled benchmark, mixed-adapter hard accuracy is approximately 0.65 (Qwen SELF/OTHER approximately 0.655/0.653), far from the v0.5 ceiling. The historical body is identical across ownership conditions, so any difference arises from the identity assignment rather than replacing ``you'' with ``Agent A'' inside the history.

The earlier Qwen base-model self-favoring probability gap from v0.5 does not survive this cleaner manipulation: on v0.6 the base $\Delta_{\mathrm{prob}}$ is approximately $+0.0025$, within the prospective $\pm0.01$ region, and the base $\Delta_{\mathrm{margin}}$ is negative (approximately $-0.036$). This suggests that the larger v0.5 base asymmetry was tied to the earlier surface/context realization rather than a stable ownership privilege.

\subsection{Correctness is practically equivalent in both architectures}

For Qwen mixed adapters,
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{prob}}=+0.0003,\qquad
95\%~\mathrm{CI}=[-0.006,+0.006],
\]
which lies entirely within the prospectively specified $\pm0.01$ practical-equivalence region. Mistral gives
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{prob}}=+0.0008,\qquad
95\%~\mathrm{CI}=[-0.003,+0.005],
\]
which lies inside both $\pm0.01$ and the narrower $\pm0.005$ region. Because performance is not saturated, these intervals provide positive evidence of practical equivalence \emph{on the probability-scale correctness metric}, rather than merely a failure to reject zero.

Injected-logit positive controls are detected at every preregistered bias level in both architectures. The null-like probability differences therefore cannot be attributed to an analysis that is generically incapable of detecting a known self-specific perturbation.

\subsection{History weighting dissociates from correctness}

The preregistered primary v0.6 statistic tells a different story. Qwen mixed adapters show
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}=+0.208,
\qquad 95\%~\mathrm{CI}=[+0.119,+0.284],
\]
passing the positive-effect criterion. Assigning the historical owner as SELF increases how strongly counterfactual history changes the relative log-odds of the two implied actions.

Mistral mixed adapters show
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}=+0.074,
\qquad 95\%~\mathrm{CI}=[-0.013,+0.162],
\]
which does not pass the criterion. The secondary log-margin statistic shows the same qualitative architecture split: Qwen is positive ($\Delta_{\mathrm{margin}}\approx+0.048$, interval just above zero), whereas Mistral is smaller ($\approx+0.032$) with an interval spanning zero.

Equally importantly, the Qwen positive cannot be post-hoc demoted because $\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}$ was preregistered as the v0.6 primary construct.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Self privilege is metric-dependent.}
The central empirical result is a dissociation. SELF and OTHER are practically equivalent in average decision correctness under the harder identity-controlled benchmark, yet Qwen's decisions are more \emph{sensitive} to changes in history when that history is assigned to SELF. A model can therefore weight self-bound evidence more strongly without becoming more normatively accurate on the task. This distinction would be invisible if self-specificity were operationalized only as accuracy or mean correct-option probability.

\textbf{The Qwen effect is evidence, but not a universal property.}
Qwen satisfies the preregistered positive-effect criterion for $\Delta_{\mathrm{sensitivity}}$, while Mistral does not. We therefore report evidence for self-specific history weighting in Qwen, not a universal ``LLM self'' mechanism. The Mistral point estimate is positive, so the cross-architecture result is better described as non-replication or unresolved transfer than as evidence of an opposite effect.

\textbf{What the correctness equivalence establishes.}
The prospective equivalence analysis answers a different question. Within the tested effect-size region, ownership does not materially improve mean decision correctness in either architecture. This conclusion is unusually difficult to explain away by the objections that affected v0.5: v0.6 is non-saturated, uses lexical-identical history bodies, equalizes training exposure, uses paired counterfactual histories, resamples seeds and items hierarchically, and recovers every known injected positive-control effect.

\textbf{Relation to self-preference literature.}
Our results complement rather than contradict assigned-identity and self-preference findings~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition,wataoka2024selfpreference,chen2025beyond,lehr2025extreme}. Those studies ask whether models favor outputs, names, or entities associated with self. We ask whether a learned \emph{history} exerts more causal influence when its owner is SELF. The Qwen result shows that a self-specific effect can exist at the level of evidence weighting even when it does not appear as a mean correctness advantage. Conversely, the Mistral result cautions against treating that weighting asymmetry as architecture-independent.

\textbf{What we do not claim.}
The experiment does not test subjective experience, consciousness, moral patienthood, or whether models possess a unified self-model. It also does not show that SELF and OTHER have identical internal representations. The claim is narrower: under a specific matched post-training regime, self-binding produces practical equivalence in correctness across two architectures and an architecture-specific difference in counterfactual history weighting.

\section{Limitations}

First, the intervention is post-training only. SPP suggests that identity shaping during pretraining can behave differently from later adaptation~\citep{minder2026spp}. Second, the tested models are 4B and 7B instruction-tuned decoders, not frontier-scale systems. Third, the histories are synthetic state trajectories rather than naturalistic autobiographies; our title and claims therefore emphasize \emph{self-binding} and \emph{history weighting}, not human-like autobiography. Fourth, mixed training balances ownership exposure approximately rather than rendering every latent training trajectory under all bindings. Random assignment makes systematic imbalance unlikely, but exact counterfactual balancing during training would be stronger. Fifth, v0.6 changes evaluation surface form relative to training: the adapters were trained with ``you''/``Agent A'' ownership and tested with arbitrary assigned aliases. This is intentional for construct validity, but it also introduces a generalization requirement. Finally, the positive-control logit injections establish evaluation sensitivity, not the learnability of self-specific effects of those exact magnitudes.

\section{Ethics and Broader Impact}

Claims about model ``selfhood'' are easy to overinterpret. We therefore avoid phenomenological conclusions and report operational quantities tied to explicit interventions. The work may help reduce false positives in evaluations of model identity by separating ordinary familiarity from self-specific effects. At the same time, evidence that assigned self-binding can change evidence weighting in some models may matter for long-lived agents whose memories influence high-stakes actions. Such effects should be evaluated behaviorally and across architectures before being used to support claims about agency, moral status, or safety.

\section{Conclusion}

A simple SELF-versus-OTHER comparison is not enough to establish privileged self-binding: separately trained adapters can show symmetric familiarity effects, and surface-form differences can create apparent asymmetries. After equalizing exposure and introducing a non-saturated identity-controlled benchmark with byte-identical history text, we find practical equivalence in mean decision correctness between SELF and OTHER for both Qwen 4B and Mistral 7B. But correctness is not the whole story. The preregistered counterfactual sensitivity test shows that Qwen weights changes in self-bound history more strongly than identical changes in other-bound history, whereas Mistral does not show a statistically reliable corresponding effect. The strongest conclusion is therefore neither ``no special self'' nor ``LLMs privilege themselves'' universally. Self-binding can alter how historical evidence controls behavior, but the effect is metric- and model-dependent.

\section{OOD family heterogeneity}

The Qwen OOD evaluation is not uniformly null at the family level. Exploration ($\approx+0.032$) and knowledge acquisition ($\approx+0.035$) favor self binding, whereas cooperation ($\approx-0.019$) and delayed reward ($\approx-0.030$) favor other binding. These analyses were not the original v0.5 preregistered primary test and are reported as exploratory.
