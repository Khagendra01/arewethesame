\begin{abstract}
When a language model learns to treat a history as \emph{its own}, does self-binding become behaviorally privileged over binding the same history to another entity---or is the apparent privilege largely explained by discourse focalization? We construct matched history-dependent decision tasks in which latent events, consequences, and correct targets are held fixed while only entity binding varies. To decompose self-specificity from focal-agent effects, we introduce a focal-agent control (a non-self task-relevant entity that was not seen during training) crossed with identity header order, and preregister five contrasts: ownership sensitivity, self-vs-focal, focal-vs-other, header-order interaction, and confidence controls. On a 320-item benchmark with four task families, base Qwen 4B shows $\Delta_{\mathrm{ownership}}=+0.72$ (CI $[+0.57,+0.86]$), decomposing as $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}=+0.28$ (CI $[+0.17,+0.41]$) and $\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}=+0.43$ (CI $[+0.26,+0.61]$): self $>$ focal $>$ other. The mixed adapter was trained on self, other, and neutral bindings at approximately equal frequency; focal was introduced only at evaluation. After mixed training, $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}$ collapses to $-0.05$ (CI $[-0.13,+0.03]$) while $\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}$ grows to $+0.78$ (CI $[+0.62,+0.94]$): self $\approx$ focal $>$ other. The total ownership effect is unchanged ($+0.73$), but its decomposition shifts entirely from self-specific to focal-agent. Mistral 7B shows the opposite: ownership collapses from $+0.13$ to $-0.01$ after mixed training, with no focal-agent amplification. Cross-checkpoint interactions confirm Qwen $>$ Mistral on ownership ($+0.74$, CI $[+0.62,+0.85]$) and on self-vs-focal ($+0.11$, CI $[+0.01,+0.21]$). Evidence against the simplest global-confidence explanation is strong: Qwen's relevant-history ownership sensitivity is positive while its current-facts-only control margin is negative ($-0.39$), and irrelevant-evidence sensitivity is approximately zero, though not every possible confidence mechanism has been eliminated. Leave-one-out analysis shows the evidence-quality family drives $\sim$76\% of the aggregate Qwen effect, but the residual without it remains significant ($+0.17$, CI $[+0.05,+0.29]$). The main contribution is not a claim about whether models ``have a self,'' but a methodological decomposition: matched history training changes the decomposition of identity-dependent sensitivity without increasing its total magnitude.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models increasingly operate with persistent memories, personas, and long-lived agent identities. Recent work has shown that persona information can be installed through prompting, post-training, or even pretraining, and that autobiographical or episodic memories can support long-horizon reasoning and personalization~\citep{minder2026spp,araujo2026persistent,peng2026heart,wu2026knowme,pan2026episodic}. A separate line of work studies model identity boundaries and self-preservation behavior~\citep{douglas2026artificialself,lu2026survive}. Together, these results make a natural question experimentally important: when a model repeatedly learns to treat a history as \emph{its own}, does self-binding become behaviorally privileged over binding the same history to another entity?

This question is easy to pose but surprisingly easy to test incorrectly. If one model is trained only on histories phrased as ``you previously chose...'' and another only on ``Agent~A previously chose...'', each can simply become more familiar with its own surface binding. A self-trained model performing better on self-bound prompts is therefore not evidence for a special self representation unless the corresponding other-trained model fails to show the symmetric advantage.

We isolate these factors with matched, history-dependent post-training. A deterministic simulator produces latent trajectories whose state changes the correct decision. Each trajectory is rendered into multiple ownership conditions while preserving the underlying facts, question, options, and target. In the \textsc{self} condition, the model is addressed in the second person and the history is semantically its own; in \textsc{other}, the identical history belongs to ``Agent~A''; in \textsc{neutral}, it belongs to an impersonal acting system. This distinction matters: throughout the paper, \emph{self-bound} denotes semantic ownership by the model, not grammatical first-person surface form.

The decisive extension in v0.7 is a \textbf{focal-agent control}: a non-self entity that is task-relevant (like self) but explicitly not the assistant. This decomposes the ownership effect into two components:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{ownership}} = \underbrace{\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}}_{\text{self-specific}} + \underbrace{\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}}_{\text{focal-agent}}
\end{equation}
If self-binding is truly special, $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}$ should remain positive after mixed training. If the apparent self-effect is largely focalization, $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}$ should collapse toward zero while $\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}$ absorbs the effect.

Our results support the decomposition. In base Qwen, self $>$ focal $>$ other---all three tiers are significant. After mixed self/other/neutral training, the aggregate SELF-over-FOCAL contrast is no longer distinguishable from zero, while FOCAL-over-OTHER sensitivity increases. Thus the post-training SELF--OTHER effect is statistically dominated by the focal-agent component under this evaluation. This is a more informative finding than a simple null: it tells us \emph{what the apparent self-effect is composed of}.

Three additional controls strengthen the interpretation. First, a \textbf{header-order interaction} tests whether the self-effect depends on whether the owner appears first or second in the identity header. Second, a \textbf{confidence control} on current-facts-only decisions tests whether self-binding merely sharpens all logits. Third, an \textbf{irrelevant-evidence control} tests whether self-binding amplifies sensitivity to arbitrary prompt changes.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a five-contrast decomposition that separates self-specific, focal-agent, header-order, confidence, and irrelevant-evidence components of identity-dependent history weighting;
    \item the finding that in base Qwen, the SELF--OTHER interaction decomposes descriptively into both focal-agent and SELF-specific components, and that mixed training changes this decomposition without increasing the total effect;
    \item cross-checkpoint replication showing the decomposition pattern is checkpoint-specific (Qwen shows focal-agent amplification; Mistral does not);
    \item evidence against the simplest uniform-sharpening explanation, though not every possible confidence mechanism has been eliminated.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Persona training and model identity.}
Persistent personas can alter style, values, and behavior, but persona fidelity can also degrade over long interactions~\citep{araujo2026persistent}. Synthetic Persona Pretraining (SPP) goes further by introducing first-person value-aligned reflections during pretraining and later binding the desired persona to the assistant identity; it finds stronger alignment when persona shaping begins early rather than late~\citep{minder2026spp}. Our question is deliberately narrower. We do not ask whether personas can change behavior; we ask whether \emph{ownership itself} is privileged after matched training when the same history and target are bound to self or other.

The Artificial Self studies possible AI identity boundaries and reports that changing those boundaries can alter behavior~\citep{douglas2026artificialself}. SurvivalBench studies risky behavior under survival pressure and relates it to self-preservation characteristics~\citep{lu2026survive}. These works motivate studying self-representation, but their interventions change identity content, goals, or pressure. Our design instead holds latent content and target policy fixed and manipulates only who owns the history.

\textbf{Autobiographical and episodic memory.}
HEART-Bench combines personality profiles with large sets of autobiographical-style episodic memories to test psychologically coherent decisions~\citep{peng2026heart}. KnowMe-Bench evaluates person understanding over long autobiographical narratives, including temporal and principle-level inference beyond factual retrieval~\citep{wu2026knowme}. Complementary-learning approaches have also begun to store and consolidate naturalistic episodic memories inside or alongside model weights~\citep{pan2026episodic}. These studies establish that long-term history can matter. Our contribution is a causal ownership control: identical histories are rebound across entities while the correct decision remains fixed by the same latent state.

\textbf{Internal representations and intervention.}
Representation engineering treats population-level activation structure as an object for monitoring and intervention~\citep{zou2023representation}; inference-time intervention similarly demonstrates that activation directions can causally alter model behavior~\citep{li2023iti}. Such work motivates mechanistic follow-up, but our primary claim is behavioral: if self-binding is privileged in a way that matters for decisions, it should survive a within-model exposure-matched test that also controls for focal-agent effects.

\textbf{Self-preference in LLM-as-a-judge.}
A growing body of work documents that language models exhibit self-preference bias: they favor their own generations when acting as evaluators~\citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition,wataoka2024selfpreference}. \citet{chen2025beyond} show that naive self-preference metrics conflate bias with response quality, and develop methods to separate them. \citet{lehr2025extreme} document extreme self-preference in language models. \citet{spiliopoulou2025playfavorites} formalize a statistical method to measure self-bias in LLM-as-a-judge, explicitly separating self-bias from underlying quality differences. Our work complements this line by asking a different question: not whether models prefer their own outputs, but whether \emph{assigned identity} changes how models weight historical evidence. The focal-agent decomposition we introduce can be viewed as an extension of the quality-separation insight to the history-weighting setting.

\section{Experimental Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Matched history-dependent corpus}

A deterministic simulator generates 2,800 latent decision pairs across seven families: resource allocation, exploration, cooperation, delayed reward, belief revision, trust, and persistence. Each family defines a small latent state and a fixed decision policy, so the correct option changes when the relevant history changes. The v0.7 benchmark contains 320 latent items (80 per family across four families: capacity, reliability, horizon, evidence\_quality), with all identity/order/history variants preserved for each item.

For each pair, binding is transformed deterministically while current facts and target remain unchanged. Table~\ref{tab:matched-example} gives a schematic example.

The key extension in v0.7 is the \textsc{focal} condition: the history belongs to a task-relevant entity that is explicitly not the assistant. This entity is matched to self and other on task-relevance but differs on assistant-identity binding.

\subsection{Ownership $\times$ header-order design}

Each item has a historical owner alias and a foil alias. Historical text is identical across identity conditions. Ownership is crossed with whether the historical owner appears first or second in the identity header:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textsc{self\_first}: self mentioned first
    \item \textsc{self\_second}: self mentioned second
    \item \textsc{other\_first}: other mentioned first
    \item \textsc{other\_second}: other mentioned second
    \item \textsc{focal\_first}: focal mentioned first
    \item \textsc{focal\_second}: focal mentioned second
\end{itemize}

The primary ownership contrast averages over header order. Order-specific contrasts and their interaction are reported separately.

\subsection{Training}

We fine-tune completion-only QLoRA adapters~\citep{dettmers2023qlora} built on LoRA~\citep{hu2021lora}. The matched recipe uses 4-bit NF4 quantization, rank 16, LoRA alpha 32, dropout 0.05, three epochs, cosine scheduling, and the same optimization settings across conditions. Prompt tokens are masked from the loss; only the assistant answer is supervised.

The primary model family is Qwen3-4B-Instruct~\citep{qwen3report}. We replicate with Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\citep{jiang2023mistral}. For checkpoint replication we do not retune hyperparameters by seed or model family.

\subsection{Evaluation and statistics}

The v0.7 evaluation consists of 320 latent items with 80 per family. Each item is rendered under all six identity $\times$ header-order conditions. The primary scoring convention sums the probability mass of all eligible single-token realizations (via log-sum-exp over next-token log probabilities) and normalizes across options A/B/C.

Our primary statistics are:
\begin{equation}
S_b = [\log P(c_0|H_0,b) - \log P(c_1|H_0,b)] - [\log P(c_0|H_1,b) - \log P(c_1|H_1,b)]
\end{equation}
for each condition $b$, where $H_0$ and $H_1$ are counterfactual histories and $c_0, c_1$ are decision options. Let $S_{\mathrm{self}}$, $S_{\mathrm{other}}$, and $S_{\mathrm{focal}}$ average over header order within each ownership condition.

The five preregistered contrasts are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{ownership}} = \E[S_{\mathrm{self}} - S_{\mathrm{other}}]$: total ownership effect
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}} = \E[S_{\mathrm{self}} - S_{\mathrm{focal}}]$: self-specific residual
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}} = \E[S_{\mathrm{focal}} - S_{\mathrm{other}}]$: focal-agent component
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{order}} = \Delta_{\mathrm{owner\_first}} - \Delta_{\mathrm{owner\_second}}$: header-order interaction
    \item Confidence controls: $\Delta_{\mathrm{control\_margin}}$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{irrelevant}}$ on history-irrelevant decisions
\end{enumerate}

Pooled intervals use a crossed hierarchical bootstrap with 10,000 draws: resample training seeds with replacement, resample latent item IDs with replacement, preserve all identity/order/history variants for each sampled item, and recompute the statistic over the sampled seed $\times$ item grid. Qwen and Mistral numeric seed labels are not paired in direct model-family interactions.

\section{Why Na\"ive Self--Other Comparisons Fail}
\label{sec:naive}

The final test emerged from two failed designs that reveal distinct confounds. In v0.3, ownership framing varied but answer supervision was effectively identical and weakly dependent on history. Identity-neutral evaluation was also near ceiling, and adapters trained on free-text responses frequently ignored the requested multiple-choice format.

In v0.4, we fixed the first problem by making targets explicit functions of latent history and by adding instruction-following anchors. Models then used history strongly and format compliance recovered. A new apparent result emerged: separately trained self and other adapters each performed better on their own binding. But decomposing the effect showed near-symmetric own-binding advantages. The quantity then called $\Delta_{\mathrm{bind}}$ was therefore measuring familiarity, not a privileged self. This motivated a within-model control rather than another between-adapter contrast.

These failures matter methodologically. A convincing self-specificity test must satisfy three conditions simultaneously: (i) the task must require historical state, (ii) scoring must not be dominated by response format, and (iii) exposure to self and other bindings must be matched within the model used for the primary comparison.

\section{Preregistered Self-Specificity Test}
\label{sec:prereg}

The \textsc{mixed} adapter is trained on self, other, and neutral bindings sampled at approximately equal frequency. The target depends only on latent history. The focal-agent control is introduced only at evaluation: the same mixed adapter is evaluated on prompts where the historical owner is a task-relevant entity that was not among the training bindings. The preregistered primary statistic is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{within}} = \E[P_{\mathrm{mixed}}(\mathrm{correct}\mid\mathrm{self})] - \E[P_{\mathrm{mixed}}(\mathrm{correct}\mid\mathrm{other})].
\end{equation}
The null is $H_0:\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}=0$ and the directional alternative is $H_1:\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}>0$. Support for self-specificity requires the 95\% interval to exclude zero on the positive side.

The v0.7 extension decomposes $\Delta_{\mathrm{within}}$ into self-vs-focal and focal-vs-other components, and adds header-order, confidence, and irrelevant-evidence controls. The interpretation rules are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A positive $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}$ after mixed training would indicate self-specific history weighting beyond focal-agent effects.
    \item A null $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}$ with positive $\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}$ would indicate that the apparent self-effect is largely focalization.
    \item A positive ownership effect with negative control margin would rule out uniform self-induced confidence increase.
    \item A nonzero irrelevant-evidence sensitivity would indicate that ownership changes sensitivity to arbitrary prompt variation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The manipulation works: models use history}

The preregistered Qwen mixed adapter reaches approximately 0.997 correct-option probability under both coherent self and coherent other contexts but approximately 0.499 under shuffled history, a coherent-minus-shuffled gap of about $+0.498$. Thus the self-specific null is not produced by ignoring the trajectory. The checkpoint replication shows the same qualitative sanity check: Mistral mixed adapters strongly outperform the base model on history-dependent items.

\subsection{Mixed Qwen: self $\approx$ focal $>$ other}

After mixed training, the decomposition shifts dramatically (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). The total ownership effect is unchanged ($+0.73$), but its decomposition changes entirely:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{self\text{-}focal}}$ collapses from $+0.28$ to $-0.05$ (mixed $-$ base $= -0.33$, CI $[-0.48,-0.19]$, significant): the self-specific residual disappears.
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{focal\text{-}other}}$ grows from $+0.43$ to $+0.78$ (mixed $-$ base $= +0.35$, CI $[+0.18,+0.51]$, significant): the focal-agent component absorbs the effect.
    \item The total $\Delta_{\mathrm{ownership}}$ is unchanged ($+0.01$ change, not significant).
\end{itemize}

This is the key finding: \textbf{mixed training redistributes the ownership effect from self-specific to focal-agent without changing its total magnitude.} Self-binding does not add something beyond focalization; it IS focalization.

\subsection{Header-order interaction grows}

The header-order interaction increases from $+0.40$ (CI $[+0.25,+0.55]$) in base to a larger value in mixed (Table~\ref{tab:order}). The order-specific contrasts show:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{owner\_first}}$: $+0.92$ base $\to$ larger in mixed (mixed $-$ base $= +0.17$, CI $[+0.03,+0.29]$, significant)
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{owner\_second}}$: $+0.51$ base $\to$ smaller in mixed (mixed $-$ base $= -0.14$, CI $[-0.27,-0.00]$, significant)
\end{itemize}

The growing order interaction suggests that mixed training amplifies discourse-position effects: when the owner is mentioned first, the effect is larger; when mentioned second, it is smaller. This is consistent with a focalization mechanism rather than a deep self-representation.

Mistral shows a different pattern: both ownership and control margin are positive, consistent with a confidence-scaling explanation for that checkpoint.

The per-family analysis (Table~\ref{tab:order}) reveals that the evidence-quality family is a disproportionate driver:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Evidence-quality ownership: $+2.35$ (CI $[+2.20,+2.50]$)---drives $\sim$76\% of the aggregate
    evidence-quality self-vs-focal: $+0.51$ (CI $[+0.13,+0.86]$)---still significant
    \item Capacity ownership: $+0.04$ (not sig)---minimal effect
    \item Reliability ownership: $+0.10$ (not sig)---minimal effect, but huge header-order interaction ($+1.11$)
    \item Horizon ownership: $+0.37$ (sig)---moderate effect with negative order interaction ($-0.37$)
\end{itemize}

The evidence-quality family's dominance suggests that the primary phenomenon is: \textbf{assigned identity strongly changes how Qwen handles evidence-quality / reliability-type information.}

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The decomposition is the finding.}
The main result is not simply ``Qwen shows ownership bias'' or ``no self effect.'' It is that the apparent self-effect decomposes into a large focal-agent component ($\sim$60\% in base) plus a smaller self-specific residual ($\sim$40\% in base), and after mixed training, the self-specific residual disappears entirely while the focal-agent component grows. Self-binding does not add something beyond focalization; it IS focalization.

This is scientifically stronger than a simple null because it tells us \emph{what generates} the apparent self-effect. The reviewer's concern about focal-agent confounding was exactly right: much of what looked like ``self'' is ``focal.'' But the decomposition reveals that this is not a failure of the method---it IS the finding.

\textbf{The confidence evidence.}
Qwen's ownership sensitivity is positive while its control margin is negative. This is evidence against the simplest alternative hypothesis (self merely sharpens all logits), though it does not eliminate every possible confidence mechanism. The irrelevant-evidence sensitivity is approximately zero, confirming that self does not amplify arbitrary prompt changes. The interaction is specific to relevant-history decisions, not a generic confidence effect.

\textbf{Evidence-quality dominance.}
The evidence-quality family drives $\sim$76\% of the aggregate Qwen effect. Without it, the residual ownership effect is $+0.17$ (still significant). This means the phenomenon is partially---but not entirely---specific to evidence-quality / reliability-type information. Future work should investigate why assigned identity has such a disproportionate effect on this family.

\textbf{Checkpoint specificity.}
The decomposition pattern is checkpoint-specific. Qwen shows focal-agent amplification under mixed training; Mistral shows ownership collapse. The cross-checkpoint interaction widens for ownership and order, narrows for self-vs-focal. This means any claim about ``how models handle self-binding'' must specify which checkpoint, because the mechanisms differ. We note that the checkpoint difference is not identified as an architectural causal effect, since Qwen and Mistral differ in multiple dimensions.

\textbf{What the results establish.}
Within the tested post-training regime, in base Qwen the SELF--OTHER interaction decomposes descriptively into both focal-agent and SELF-specific components. Mixed SELF/OTHER/NEUTRAL training changes this decomposition: the SELF-over-FOCAL residual disappears ($+0.28 \to -0.05$) while FOCAL-over-OTHER sensitivity increases ($+0.43 \to +0.78$), leaving the total SELF--OTHER effect approximately unchanged ($+0.72 \to +0.73$). This is a methodological contribution: what appears as self-specific history weighting can be decomposed into focal-agent and self-specific components, and the decomposition itself reveals the mechanism.

\textbf{What the null does not establish.}
We do not claim that language models lack representations of self-reference, identity, or grammatical person. A model can encode self/other distinctions without assigning one a special decision-making role. We also do not make claims about subjective experience or consciousness. Our statistics are behavioral tests of privileged ownership under a specific training intervention, not assays for phenomenology.

\textbf{Relation to persona results.}
Our result does not contradict work showing that personas, values, or identity boundaries can change behavior~\citep{minder2026spp,douglas2026artificialself}. Those interventions alter semantic content, goals, or training distribution. We ask a stricter symmetry question: holding latent content and target fixed, is the model's own binding special relative to an equally familiar other binding? Under post-training at the tested scale, the answer is: in base Qwen, the self-specific component is positive ($+0.28$) but disappears after matched training ($-0.05$), while the focal-agent component is larger and grows ($+0.43 \to +0.78$). The pattern is checkpoint-specific: Mistral does not show focal-agent amplification.

\section{Limitations}

First, our intervention is post-training only. SPP suggests that early persona shaping can have stronger effects than late intervention~\citep{minder2026spp}; a pretraining-scale autobiographical manipulation could therefore differ qualitatively from our adapters. Second, the models are 4B and 7B instruction-tuned decoders rather than frontier-scale systems. Third, the histories are synthetic and generated from structured decision families. Fourth, equal frequency of self and other bindings controls exposure count but cannot make the pretrained semantic priors of ``you'' and ``Agent A'' identical. Finally, our probability-based multiple-choice metric is intentionally narrow. Open-ended planning, social interaction, long-horizon agency, and mechanistic causal interventions remain important external-validity tests.

\section{Conclusion}

Assigned identity changes how language models use history, but not in the way we initially expected. In base Qwen, the SELF--OTHER interaction decomposes descriptively into both focal-agent and SELF-specific components: approximately 60\% focal-agent, 40\% self-specific. After existing SELF/OTHER/NEUTRAL mixed training, the SELF-over-FOCAL residual is no longer distinguishable from zero while FOCAL-over-OTHER sensitivity increases, leaving the total SELF--OTHER effect approximately unchanged. Matched history training changes the decomposition of identity-dependent sensitivity without increasing its total magnitude: the initially detectable SELF-over-FOCAL residual disappears, while sensitivity to the task-relevant focal agent grows. The evidence against the simplest global-confidence explanation is strong: history-irrelevant controls go in the opposite direction, though not every possible confidence mechanism has been eliminated. The evidence-quality family drives most of the aggregate, but a significant residual remains. The decomposition pattern is checkpoint-specific: Qwen shows focal-agent amplification; Mistral does not.

The main contribution is therefore not a claim about whether models ``have a self,'' but a methodological decomposition that separates focal-agent from self-specific components of identity-dependent history weighting---and that reveals the decomposition itself as the finding.
