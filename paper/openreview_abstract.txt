Title
No Special "I"? Self-Binding Changes History Weighting Without Reliably Improving Decisions in Language Models

Abstract
We ask whether binding a learned history to a language model's own identity gives that history privileged influence over subsequent decisions. We construct matched history-dependent tasks in which latent events, decision targets, and supervision are held fixed while ownership binding varies. Initial experiments show that separately trained self and other adapters each prefer their familiar binding, motivating a preregistered mixed-binding control that equalizes exposure within the same model. Across five Qwen 4B and five Mistral 7B training seeds, self and other yield nearly identical mean decision correctness on held-out in-distribution data. We then introduce a harder 840-item identity-controlled benchmark that removes ceiling effects and holds historical text byte-identical across self and other conditions, changing only which arbitrary alias is assigned as the model's identity. On this benchmark, the prospectively specified probability-scale self effect is practically equivalent within a ±1 percentage-point region for both architectures. However, a preregistered counterfactual history-sensitivity measure reveals a dissociation: Qwen assigns significantly greater causal log-odds influence to self-bound history (Δ_sensitivity = +0.208, 95% CI [+0.119, +0.284]), whereas the corresponding Mistral effect is smaller and its interval spans zero (+0.074, [-0.013, +0.162]). Thus, self-binding does not robustly improve decision correctness, but it can alter how strongly historical evidence controls behavior in a model-dependent manner. These results distinguish history use, binding familiarity, decision performance, and self-specific history weighting.
