Title
Does Assigned Identity Change How Language Models Use History? Controlled Tests of Self-Binding

Abstract
We test whether assigning the same historical record to a language model's own identity changes its subsequent forced-choice behavior after matched history-dependent post-training. Separately trained SELF and OTHER adapters show own-binding advantages, motivating a mixed-binding control that gives a single model approximately balanced exposure to self, other, and neutral histories. On the original preregistered in-distribution evaluation, mixed adapters show no detected SELF advantage in mean normalized designated-option score across five Qwen 4B and five Mistral 7B training seeds. We then preregister a harder 840-item identity-controlled evaluation in which SELF and OTHER share byte-identical history bodies, differ only in which arbitrary alias is assigned as the assistant, and each item contains two coherent counterfactual histories that imply different simulator-policy actions. The primary statistic is an ownership-by-counterfactual-history log-odds interaction, Delta_sensitivity. Qwen is positive (+0.208, 95% CI [+0.119, +0.284]), whereas Mistral is smaller and inconclusive (+0.074, [-0.013, +0.162]); the preregistered criterion for a robust effect in both model families is therefore not met. Separately, the mean normalized designated-policy option score satisfies the prospectively specified +/-0.01 equivalence criterion in both families. These results distinguish binding familiarity, average policy agreement, and counterfactual log-odds sensitivity. The primary interaction is real at the behavioral-score level but does not by itself identify a unique internal evidence-weighting mechanism.
